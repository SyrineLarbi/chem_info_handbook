Foundation models and the frontiers. Pretraining, materials, robots, and keeping it all alive.

Four subjects that share one idea: stop training from scratch. A model pretrained on a hundred million molecules already knows chemistry you do not have labels for. A robot that chooses its own next experiment learns faster than a plan. And a model nobody monitors quietly stops being true — which is the part of this volume that will matter to you first.

Part I

Foundation models

Lesson 01

Pretraining, and why it works at all

Volume 11 used a SMILES model to generate. The same trained model can be used a second way, which turns out to be more broadly useful: as a source of representations.

  THE PROBLEM IT SOLVES

    Labelled chemical data is tiny and expensive. A typical
    project has 200-2,000 measured molecules, because each one
    cost a real experiment.

    UNLABELLED chemical data is enormous and free. PubChem and
    ZINC hold hundreds of millions of structures with no
    measurements attached at all.

  THE MOVE

    1. PRETRAIN on 100M unlabelled SMILES, with no chemistry
       objective — just "predict the masked token" or "predict
       the next token", exactly as BERT and GPT learn English.

    2. The model is forced to learn valence, aromaticity, ring
       systems and functional group patterns, because those are
       what make the next token predictable.

    3. NOW USE IT on your 200 labelled molecules — either as a
       feature extractor, or by fine-tuning.

  ⭐ THE CLAIM WORTH REMEMBERING
     Embeddings from a pretrained model plus a simple classifier,
     on 50-200 labelled molecules, can rival a fingerprint model
     trained on thousands. That is the whole value proposition:
     PRETRAINING BUYS YOU DATA YOU DO NOT HAVE.
Where you have seen this before

This is the same argument as Volume 10's, one level up. A GNN learns a representation from your task's data; a foundation model learns one from all chemistry and then adapts. Which is why the deciding question is identical in both cases — how much labelled data do you actually have?

Lesson 02

Using embeddings — and checking them first

Worked example · the check nobody runs 
THE EASY PATH
    molecule -> pretrained model -> a vector (say 768 numbers)
             -> logistic regression / random forest -> prediction

    No GPU, no fine-tuning, ten lines of code. Often very good.

⭐ BUT FIRST: DOES THE MODEL KNOW YOUR CHEMISTRY?

    A model pretrained on ZINC drug-like molecules has seen
    almost no organometallics, no polymers, no molecules with
    rare elements, no inorganic salts. On those, its embeddings
    are meaningless — AND IT WILL NOT TELL YOU. You get a vector
    either way, and the downstream model trains happily on noise.

THE FIVE-MINUTE TEST

    1. embed 500 of YOUR molecules
    2. PCA or t-SNE to 2D
    3. colour by something you know — scaffold, series, activity

    WHAT YOU WANT TO SEE
       structurally similar molecules sitting together.
       your series forming recognisable groups.

    WHAT MEANS STOP
       a featureless blob with no structure at all.
       The model has not seen your chemistry. Use fingerprints.

ALSO: HOW TO POOL
    Many models are pretrained with masked-language-modelling
    only, which never trains the [CLS] token to mean anything.
    Taking [CLS] from such a model gives worse results than
    MEAN POOLING over the token embeddings.
    -> default to mean pooling unless the model was explicitly
       pretrained with a [CLS] objective.
Lesson 03

Fine-tuning without destroying what it learned

The failure mode

Catastrophic forgetting. You fine-tune on 300 molecules at a normal learning rate, the model overfits them within two epochs, and in doing so overwrites the general chemical knowledge that was the entire reason you used it. Validation loss improves briefly, then diverges.

The settings that prevent it
  • A small learning rate on the encoder — around 1e-5, far lower than you would use training from scratch.
  • Warmup over roughly the first 10% of steps.
  • Watch training and validation loss per epoch. Divergence after one or two epochs is the signature.
  • Consider LoRA — train small adapter matrices and leave the pretrained weights frozen. Cheap, and structurally immune to this failure.
The benchmarking rule that decides the answer

Scaffold split, not random split. A random split leaks structural information — near-identical analogues land in both train and test — and foundation models look spectacular under it, then collapse on novel scaffolds in deployment. Report both, and show the gap; the gap is the honest number.

Part II

LLMs in chemistry

Lesson 05

The one thing an LLM must never do

Never let a language model compute a chemical fact

Ask a general LLM "is this molecule drug-like?" and it will answer confidently — without computing the molecular weight or logP. Ask it for a SMILES string and it will produce one that looks entirely plausible and is invalid 10–25% of the time: an unclosed ring, an impossible valence, a bracket that does not match. Downstream, that string either fails silently or parses into a different molecule than intended.

  THE RULE

    An LLM may DECIDE WHAT TO COMPUTE.
    RDKit DOES THE COMPUTING.

  AND THE NON-NEGOTIABLE STEP

    every SMILES string that comes out of a language model
    goes straight into Chem.MolFromSmiles() before ANYTHING
    else touches it. None -> discard, log, move on.

    This is three lines of code and it is the difference
    between a pipeline and a rumour.
Lesson 06

The tool-augmented pattern

Worked example · the right division of labour 
USER ASKS
    "Is this molecule likely to cross the blood-brain barrier,
     and if not, what would you change?"

WRONG — the LLM answers from memory
    "Yes, this molecule has good BBB penetration."
    Confident. Fluent. Possibly wrong. No calculation happened.

RIGHT — the LLM orchestrates, the tools compute

    1. LLM decides which tools to call
           -> rdkit.Descriptors.MolWt
           -> rdkit.Crippen.MolLogP
           -> rdkit.rdMolDescriptors.CalcTPSA
           -> your trained BBB classifier

    2. TOOLS return real numbers
           MW 412.5    logP 4.8    TPSA 92.4    P(BBB) 0.21

    3. LLM interprets and explains
           "TPSA of 92 is above the ~90 threshold usually
            associated with BBB penetration, and the model gives
            0.21. Reducing polar surface area — for instance
            replacing the free carboxylic acid with an ester or
            an amide — is the usual move."

⭐ WHAT THE LLM IS ACTUALLY GOOD FOR HERE
   · turning a vague question into a concrete set of calculations
   · explaining a result to a chemist in their language
   · writing the RDKit code to do something you described
   · reading and summarising literature

   Every one of those is a LANGUAGE task. None is a CHEMISTRY
   calculation. Keep that line and LLMs are genuinely useful;
   blur it and you will publish something embarrassing.
Part III

Beyond drug molecules

Try it · You have 180 labelled compounds for a rare endpoint. A colleague proposes fine-tuning a foundation model; another wants a random forest on ECFP4. Who is right?
Answer

At 180 compounds, try the foundation model's embeddings with a simple classifier first — but run the random forest too, because it costs an hour and settles the argument with a number.

WHY EMBEDDINGS RATHER THAN FINE-TUNING
   180 examples is in the band where pretrained knowledge
   substitutes for data you do not have — but it is also few
   enough that FINE-TUNING will overfit within two epochs
   and destroy the knowledge you came for. Embeddings +
   logistic regression has no such failure mode.

BEFORE ANY OF IT — THE FIVE-MINUTE CHECK
   Embed the 180, project to 2D, colour by series. If they
   cluster sensibly, the model has seen this chemistry and
   the embeddings mean something. If it is a featureless
   blob, the answer is fingerprints and the debate is over.

AND WHATEVER YOU CHOOSE
   Scaffold split. With 180 compounds a random split will
   flatter BOTH approaches enormously, and you will pick the
   wrong one on the strength of a number that is not real.

The meta-answer worth giving in an interview: "at this data size the representation matters more than the model, and the split matters more than either."

Lesson 07

Materials — where your usual tools stop working

Everything so far assumed a molecule: a finite set of atoms with a definite structure. A crystal is neither finite nor definite in that sense, and that single difference invalidates most of the toolkit.

  A MOLECULE                    A CRYSTAL

  finite. 30 atoms, done.       infinite, by repetition of a
                                UNIT CELL in three dimensions

  has a SMILES string           has no SMILES. It has a unit
                                cell (lattice vectors) plus the
                                positions of atoms inside it

  graph: atoms and bonds        graph: atoms and bonds — but an
                                atom near the cell edge BONDS TO
                                ATOMS IN THE NEXT CELL ALONG

  ⭐ PERIODICITY IS THE WHOLE DIFFERENCE.
     A crystal graph must carry the bonds that cross the cell
     boundary, or you have modelled a lonely fragment rather
     than a material. This is why molecular GNNs do not simply
     transfer, and why CGCNN, MEGNet and ALIGNN exist.

  AND THE PROPERTIES ARE DIFFERENT TOO
     not logP and solubility, but formation energy, band gap,
     bulk modulus, conductivity — properties OF THE BULK, which
     no single molecule has.
Lesson 08

Polymers, and why SMILES fails them

The problem

SMILES describes an exact structure. A polymer is stochastic: a sample of polyethylene is a mixture of chains of different lengths, and a copolymer has monomers in a random order. There is no single exact structure to write down.

BigSMILES

Extends SMILES with curly braces to denote a stochastic object — "this repeat unit, many times, with these end groups" — rather than pretending a polymer is one big molecule.

The pragmatic approach

In practice most polymer ML works on the repeat unit as an ordinary molecule, computes normal descriptors from it, and predicts bulk properties from those. Crude, and it works surprisingly well — the classic target being glass transition temperature (Tg), which decides whether a polymer is glassy or rubbery in use.

What it cannot capture

Chain length distribution, tacticity, branching, crystallinity and processing history — all of which genuinely change the material. A repeat-unit model that predicts Tg well is predicting a Tg, not the one your particular sample will have.

Lesson 09

The materials databases

DatabaseWhat is in itWhat to watch for
Materials ProjectComputed properties for well over a hundred thousand inorganic crystals — formation energies, band gaps, elastic properties. The standard starting point, with a good API.Almost everything is computed, not measured. Band gaps from standard DFT are systematically underestimated — a well-known, roughly predictable bias.
OQMDAnother large DFT database, strong on formation energies and phase stability.Different DFT settings from Materials Project. Do not mix values across databases without checking the method.
AFLOWVery large automated DFT repository with extensive structural prototypes.Same caution. Automated generation means less curation.
Open CatalystEnormous set of catalyst surface–adsorbate calculations, built explicitly for ML.Built as an ML benchmark; strong for method development, narrower as chemistry.
The trap that is specific to this field

In drug discovery your labels are measurements. In materials, most labels are DFT calculations — so a model trained on them learns to reproduce DFT, including its systematic errors. A model with an R² of 0.98 against Materials Project band gaps has learned to imitate a calculation that is itself known to be biased low against experiment. "Accurate" here means "agrees with the simulation", which is not the same claim as "agrees with reality", and you must say which one you mean.

Self-driving labs

  THE CLOSED LOOP

      ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │                                              │
      v                                              │
   MODEL proposes the next experiments                │
      |                                              │
      v                                              │
   ROBOT runs them  (liquid handler, plate reader,   │
      |              flow chemistry rig)             │

             and updates the model

  ⭐ WHAT CHANGES: the bottleneck moves. In a normal lab, ideas
     are cheap and experiments are slow. Here experiments are
     fast, so the limiting factor becomes CHOOSING WELL — which
     is why active learning (next lesson) is the heart of it.

  WHAT ACTUALLY BREAKS IN PRACTICE
     not the science — the PLUMBING. Instrument files in a dozen
     vendor formats, a plate map that does not match the sample
     list, a robot that silently skipped a well, units that
     changed between runs. Most of the engineering in a
     self-driving lab is data integration, and that is where a
     cheminformatician spends their time.
Lesson 11

Active learning — choosing the next experiment

Worked example · why the best-predicted experiment is often the wrong one 
YOU CAN RUN 24 REACTIONS THIS WEEK. WHICH 24?

STRATEGY A — pure exploitation
    pick the 24 the model predicts will work best.
    -> you confirm what you already believed.
    -> the model learns almost nothing, because it was already
       confident about those.

STRATEGY B — pure exploration
    pick the 24 the model is most uncertain about.
    -> the model learns a lot.
    -> you get no good results this week, and your chemist
       stops returning your calls.

STRATEGY C — BAYESIAN OPTIMISATION (what is actually used)
    The model gives a PREDICTION and an UNCERTAINTY for every
    candidate. An acquisition function trades them off:

      "expected improvement" — how much better than the best
      result so far might this be, ACCOUNTING for uncertainty?

    A point predicted mediocre but wildly uncertain can beat a
    point predicted good and certain, because it MIGHT be
    excellent and you would learn either way.

⭐ THE CHEMISTRY-SPECIFIC COMPLICATIONS
   · MIXED VARIABLES — temperature is continuous, solvent is
     categorical, and the model must handle both
   · BATCH SELECTION — you run a plate of 24 at once, so you
     need 24 points that are informative TOGETHER, not 24
     copies of the single most informative point
   · CONSTRAINTS — you cannot run at 150 C in a solvent that
     boils at 80 C, and the optimiser must know that
Lesson 12

ELNs, LIMS and instrument data

What they are

The ELN (Electronic Lab Notebook) is where chemists record what they did; the LIMS tracks samples, plates and assay results. Benchling and Dotmatics are the common platforms, and both expose REST APIs. Together they are the data backbone of a wet lab — and usually the real source of your training data.

Why this lands on you

Because the data arrives in whatever shape the instrument emitted. Plate readers, LC-MS systems and NMR spectrometers each write their own vendor format, and none of it is analysis-ready. Writing robust parsers for instrument output is unglamorous and is frequently the highest-value thing a cheminformatician does in a wet-lab organisation.

The habit worth forming

Keep the raw instrument file, always, unmodified — and derive everything from it in code. When an assay result is questioned eighteen months later, the parsed table will not settle the argument and the raw file will. This is the same principle as Volume 06's rule about never cleaning in place.

Lesson 13

Building tools chemists will actually use

The adoption rule

A model behind an API that a chemist cannot reach is a model that does not exist. A mediocre model in a tool people use beats an excellent model in a notebook nobody can run. Streamlit and Gradio exist for exactly this, and an afternoon spent on an interface routinely outperforms a week spent on accuracy.

  WHAT A CHEMIST-FACING TOOL MUST DO

   1. TAKE INPUT THE WAY THEY HAVE IT
        a sketcher, a pasted SMILES, an uploaded SD file.
        Not "provide a canonical SMILES via JSON".

   2. SHOW THE MOLECULE
        drawn, not as a string. They verify by looking.

   3. GIVE A NUMBER AND A CONFIDENCE
        ⭐ and an APPLICABILITY DOMAIN FLAG — "this compound is
        outside what the model has seen" is the single most
        useful thing the interface can say, and the thing most
        internal tools omit.

   4. EXPLAIN, A LITTLE
        which features drove it, or the nearest training
        molecules. "0.82" alone does not get acted on.

   5. BE HONEST WHEN IT DOES NOT KNOW
        a refusal is trusted. A confident wrong answer, once
        discovered, ends the tool's career.

  THE SHAPE THAT WORKS
     FastAPI service holding the model + a thin Streamlit or
     Gradio front end. The service is testable and reusable;
     the front end is disposable and that is fine.
Part V

Keeping models alive

Lesson 14

Why chemical drift is not like other drift

The difference that matters

In most ML, distribution drift is gradual — user behaviour shifts over months. In drug discovery it is abrupt, and it is caused by the project succeeding. Hit finding explores diverse scaffolds; lead optimisation then narrows onto one series and makes small changes to it. The day the project moves from one phase to the next, the input distribution changes overnight and your applicability domain no longer covers the compounds being submitted.

Scaffold shift
A new chemical series appears whose core is absent from training data. The most common and most damaging form.
Property range expansion
Optimisation pushes compounds beyond the range the model ever saw — you are asking it to extrapolate precisely where you most want to be right.
Functional group novelty
A chemist introduces a group that never appeared in training.
Target switch
Someone reuses your model for a different biological target. The chemistry looks similar; the SAR is unrelated. This is a governance failure more than a technical one.
And the cost asymmetry

A bad recommendation in an e-commerce model shows someone an irrelevant advert. A bad prediction here sends a chemist to the bench for a week — on the order of $5,000–$10,000 of synthesis effort per compound in a pharma setting — and, if it points a programme in the wrong direction, costs months. That asymmetry is why applicability-domain reporting is not optional in this field, however unusual it looks to an ML engineer arriving from another industry.

Lesson 15

Versioning what actually matters

  A MODEL IS NOT REPRODUCIBLE UNLESS ALL FIVE ARE PINNED

   1. CODE            git. The easy one, and the only one most
                      teams do.

   2. DATA            ⭐ THE ONE THAT IS ALWAYS MISSED.
                      "ChEMBL" is not a version. ChEMBL 33 is.
                      The database updates quarterly and your
                      training set changes underneath you.
                      -> DVC, or a hashed immutable snapshot

   3. THE SPLIT       the exact train/test assignment. A
                      scaffold split with a different random
                      seed is a different experiment, and
                      "we get 0.82" is not reproducible without it.

   4. ENVIRONMENT     ⭐ AND RDKit'S VERSION SPECIFICALLY.
                      Descriptor implementations and aromaticity
                      perception change between releases. The
                      same molecule can yield different
                      fingerprints under different RDKit
                      versions — silently.

   5. THE MODEL       weights plus hyperparameters plus the
                      metrics it was accepted on.
Lesson 16

Monitoring, and the model card

Worked example · what to monitor for a deployed QSAR model 
DAILY / PER PREDICTION
    · applicability domain coverage
      what fraction of today's submissions fall inside the
      training distribution?
      ⭐ THIS IS THE LEADING INDICATOR. It falls WEEKS before
         accuracy does, because AD coverage degrades as soon as
         the chemistry moves — while accuracy cannot be measured
         until the assay results come back.
    · prediction distribution — a sudden shift in the histogram
      of outputs usually means the inputs changed
    · scaffold novelty rate — how many submissions carry a core
      never seen in training?

WHEN ASSAY RESULTS ARRIVE (weeks later)
    · actual accuracy on the compounds that were predicted
    · accuracy INSIDE versus OUTSIDE the applicability domain,
      reported separately — if they are the same, your AD
      definition is not doing anything

QUARTERLY
    · retrain on the new data. ChEMBL updates; internal
      screening adds results weekly.
    · re-run the whole evaluation, not just the new part

THE MODEL CARD — one page, versioned with the model
    what it predicts · what it must NOT be used for · training
    data and its version · the split strategy · metrics, with
    the split they were measured under · the applicability
    domain definition · known failure modes · owner and date

⭐ THE "MUST NOT BE USED FOR" LINE IS THE MOST VALUABLE ONE,
   because a model gets reused by someone who was not in the
   room. Writing "not validated for covalent inhibitors or for
   targets other than X" takes ten seconds and prevents the
   single most common governance failure in this field.

Lesson 18

Multimodal — models that read and write about molecules

What they do

Text2Mol and MolT5 (Edwards et al., 2022) translate in both directions between SMILES and natural-language descriptions: molecule captioning turns a structure into prose, and the reverse turns a description into a candidate structure.

Where this is genuinely useful
  • Retrieval. Searching a corpus by describing what you want — "a selective CDK4/6 inhibitor with a fluorinated aromatic" — rather than by substructure.
  • Linking structures to the literature that discusses them, which is a real and tedious task.
  • Annotating large libraries with human-readable summaries.
Where it is oversold

Text-to-molecule generation reads like magic in a demo and is unreliable in practice: the output is a SMILES string subject to the same 10–25% invalidity as any language-model output, with no guarantee the molecule matches the description. It is a retrieval and annotation tool, not a design tool.

The rule, unchanged

Validate every generated string with RDKit before anything downstream touches it — and let the model decide what to compute rather than computing it.

Rapid drill

One-sentence answers to have ready
Why does pretraining on unlabelled SMILES help?
  Labelled chemistry is tiny and expensive; unlabelled is huge
  and free. Predicting masked tokens forces the model to learn
  valence, aromaticity and functional groups for free.

How do I know a pretrained model covers my chemistry?
  Embed your molecules, project to 2D, colour by scaffold. If
  similar molecules cluster, fine. If it is a shapeless blob,
  the model has not seen your chemistry — use fingerprints.

Foundation model or task-specific model?
  By dataset size. <100 molecules: embeddings. 100-1,000: LoRA.
  1,000-10,000: compare both. >10,000: task-specific usually wins.

Why is fine-tuning so easy to get wrong?
  Catastrophic forgetting. Use ~1e-5 on the encoder, warmup, and
  watch validation loss diverge after 1-2 epochs.

Random or scaffold split?
  Scaffold, always, for the headline number — random split leaks
  structure and flatters pretrained models. Report both.

What must an LLM never do?
  Compute a chemical fact. It decides WHAT to compute; RDKit
  computes it. And every LLM SMILES goes through MolFromSmiles.

What makes a crystal different from a molecule?
  Periodicity. It is infinite by repetition, has no SMILES, and
  atoms bond across the unit cell boundary — which is why
  molecular GNNs do not transfer.

Why is BigSMILES needed?
  Polymers are stochastic — variable chain lengths, random
  copolymer sequences. SMILES describes an exact structure.

What is the catch with materials databases?
  Most labels are DFT calculations, not measurements. A high R2
  means you reproduce the simulation, bias included.

Exploration or exploitation?
  Neither alone. Bayesian optimisation trades predicted value
  against uncertainty — and in a lab you need BATCH selection
  and real constraints too.

Why is chemical drift different?
  It is abrupt, not gradual. Moving from hit finding to lead
  optimisation changes the input distribution overnight.

What do you monitor on a deployed QSAR model?
  Applicability-domain coverage, daily. It falls weeks before
  accuracy, which cannot be measured until assays return.
