Part I

The portfolio

Lesson 01

Portfolio over degrees

Why the claim holds in this field specifically

Cheminformatics hiring is unusually evidence-driven, because the work is unusually easy to check. A public repository with a working QSAR platform, an honest scaffold-split number and a deployed demo is directly inspectable in a way a degree is not. This does not make credentials worthless — it means an artefact outranks a claim, and you can start building artefacts this week.

The shape that works

Three finished projects beat twelve abandoned ones. Prediction, screening, generation — the three capstones — on one target, so they read as a programme rather than three unrelated exercises.

The mistake that keeps people invisible

Perfectionism before publishing. The project that is nearly ready for two years helps nobody. A finished, honest, slightly rough project that exists beats an excellent one that does not.

The test

Can a stranger understand what you built and why it is good without running it? If not, the problem is the presentation, not the work.

Lesson 02

The README that gets read

In this order — and the order is the whole trick
1. RESULTS FIRST.
   "Predicts hERG inhibition. ROC-AUC 0.81 on a scaffold split
    (0.93 on a random split — see below for why that gap
    matters)."

   ⭐ NOT "this project uses RDKit and scikit-learn". Nobody
      has ever been impressed by a list of imports.

2. A SCREENSHOT OR A GIF, immediately.
   It must be visible without cloning anything.

3. MOTIVATION — two sentences on the decision this changes.

4. METHODS — enough to judge the work. The split strategy,
   the applicability domain, the baseline you beat.

5. ⭐ LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK.
   "Where this fails: outside the applicability domain, and on
    covalent binders, which are absent from the training set."

   Almost nobody writes this section. It is the single
   fastest way to signal that you understand your own work,
   and reviewers notice its absence more than they notice
   its presence.

6. INSTALLATION — one command. If it takes four, fix the
   project, not the README.

7. USAGE — one worked example with real output shown.

8. LICENSE and ACKNOWLEDGMENTS. Cite the data source and its
   version. In this field, provenance is professional
   courtesy and also evidence you know data has versions.
Try it · Your model gets ROC-AUC 0.93 on a random split and 0.81 on a scaffold split. Which goes in the README headline?
Answer

0.81 — and put 0.93 next to it, with one sentence explaining the gap.

Leading with 0.93 is not a lie, but any reviewer who knows the field will check your split, find it was random, and silently discount everything else in the repository. You would have spent your credibility to buy a number nobody believes.

Leading with 0.81 and explaining the gap does the opposite: it demonstrates you know why the numbers differ, which is exactly the competence the job requires. The sentence to write is something like:

"ROC-AUC 0.81 (scaffold split) / 0.93 (random split).
 The gap is the point: random splits put close analogues on
 both sides, so 0.93 measures how well the model recognises
 series it has already seen. 0.81 is what to expect on new
 chemistry, which is what the model is for."

That paragraph is worth more than the model.

Lesson 03

The profile around the projects

GitHub

Pin the good repositories. Every repository has a README — a bare one reads as abandoned. A commit history showing steady work beats a single "initial commit" dropping someone else's notebook, because the history is evidence the work is yours.

LinkedIn, used the way this field uses it

Not a CV transcript. A headline naming the intersection — "cheminformatics · molecular ML · RDKit" — and posts that are short write-ups of things you built or read. In a field this small, a handful of substantive posts makes you recognisable faster than any amount of profile polish.

Engaging with researchers

The community is genuinely small and unusually accessible. Reading someone's paper and asking a specific, informed question is a normal way to become known. Specific is the operative word — "I enjoyed your paper" is noise; "your applicability-domain definition uses distance-to-model rather than leverage, was that for the non-linear case?" is a conversation.

The honest caution

Building a profile is worth a few hours a month, not a few hours a week. The artefacts are the asset; the profile only makes them findable.

Lesson 05

Your first contribution

  THE ORDER THAT ACTUALLY WORKS

  1. DOCUMENTATION AND DOCSTRINGS
       Genuinely valued, permanently needed, and it forces you
       to read the code carefully enough to understand it.
       ⭐ Improving a docstring means working out exactly what
          a function does — which is the real benefit.

  2. TESTS
       Scientific code is chronically under-tested. Writing a
       test forces you to state the expected behaviour
       precisely, and a test for an edge case you found
       yourself is a genuinely valuable contribution.

  3. "GOOD FIRST ISSUE"
       Maintainers label these deliberately for newcomers.
       Comment before you start, so two people do not do the
       same work.

  4. BUG FIXES, then features.

  ⚠ WHAT NOT TO OPEN WITH
     A large refactor of code you met last week. It will not
     be merged, and it costs a maintainer an afternoon to
     explain why.

  ⭐ THE SIDE EFFECT THAT MATTERS MORE THAN THE MERGE
     Reading a mature scientific codebase shows you how
     careful people handle aromaticity perception,
     stereochemistry and numerical edge cases. That reading
     changes how you write your own code — which is worth
     more than the commit.

The market's actual shape

There are more open positions than qualified candidates, and the reason employers give is consistent: they need chemistry plus real software engineering plus ML in one person. Chemistry-only candidates cannot build the systems; ML-only candidates cannot ask the right questions. The intersection is the whole opportunity, and it is what this course has been assembling.

The areas currently hiring hardest: generative molecular design · multi-endpoint ADMET with uncertainty · foundation models for chemistry · automated synthesis planning · protein–ligand co-design.

Lesson 08

Remote work, and working from Tunisia

Where remote actually exists

AI biotech startups and tech companies are the realistic remote employers — many are fully distributed. Big pharma has largely returned to hybrid and rarely hires new people fully remote. CROs are mostly on-site.

Two structural advantages worth naming explicitly 
1  THE TIMEZONE. Tunisia is CET — the SAME clock as Paris.

      Tunis 09:00  =  Paris 09:00  =  Basel 09:00
                   =  London 08:00 =  Berlin 09:00

   Full overlap with the entire European working day. That
   is a decisive advantage over remote candidates in the
   Americas or East Asia, and it should appear in every
   application rather than being left to be discovered.

2  FRENCH. It opens French pharma in a way most
   international candidates cannot match:
      Sanofi · Servier · Pierre Fabre · Ipsen · bioMerieux
   all with computational chemistry teams.
The honest constraints

Few cheminformatics roles exist locally · visas complicate on-site EU and US positions · US West Coast overlap is poor at 8–9 hours · and networking opportunities for this specific field are limited in-country, which is part of why the open-source route matters more here than elsewhere.

The target to aim at

EU-remote at an AI biotech, or a computational team at French pharma. Plan around the timezone you have rather than against it.

Lesson 09

Freelancing and CRO partnerships

The alternative pathway

Not every route is an employment contract. Consulting for several organisations at once is a real option in this field, and it is often easier to start than a first full-time role internationally.

How it works in practice
  • A consulting profile specialising in something nameable — "cheminformatics consulting", "molecular ML" — rather than general data science.
  • European CROs that subcontract computational work — Evotec, Pharmacelera, Iktos — are the natural targets.
  • Offer specific services, not availability: QSAR model building, virtual screening campaigns, chemical data curation, pipeline development. A named deliverable is far easier to buy than hours.
  • A lower cost of living lets you price competitively while still earning well — a genuine structural advantage, and not one to be shy about.
Why it is worth considering early

Two or three delivered consulting projects are themselves portfolio evidence, and they solve the problem every first-time applicant has: somebody paid for this work is a stronger statement than any personal project.

The interview

Lesson 10

The technical rounds

  WHAT A CHEMINFORMATICS LOOP USUALLY CONTAINS

  1. SCREEN            background, motivation, what you built
  2. CODING            RDKit-flavoured. Parse, standardise,
                       compute, filter. ⭐ Usually not
                       algorithms-and-data-structures — they
                       want to see you handle molecules.
  3. ML / STATS        validation, splits, metrics, leakage.
                       This is where most candidates are lost.
  4. DOMAIN            chemistry reasoning. They are checking
                       you will not say something absurd to a
                       medicinal chemist.
  5. PROJECT DEEP DIVE your own work, interrogated. ⭐ This is
                       why the four-sentence write-up from
                       Volume 14 exists.
  6. BEHAVIOURAL       collaboration, conflict, communication.

  ⭐ THE PATTERN THAT WINS ACROSS ALL OF THEM
     NAME THE TRAP BEFORE YOU NAME THE METHOD.
     "The risk here is leakage through near-duplicate
      analogues, so the first thing I would do is..."
     That sentence signals experience more reliably than any
     technique you could list.
Lesson 11

The questions that decide it

What a strong answer contains
"How would you validate a QSAR model?"
  ⭐ THE SCREENING QUESTION OF THIS ENTIRE FIELD.
  Scaffold split · applicability domain · y-randomisation ·
  performance reported inside and outside the domain · a
  baseline. If you answer "80/20 random split and
  cross-validation", the interview is effectively over and
  the rest is politeness.

"Your model has R2 of 0.92. Are you happy?"
  The correct reaction is SUSPICION. Ask about the split,
  check for duplicates across train and test, check for
  leakage, ask whether the test set is one narrow series.

"What is an activity cliff and why do you care?"
  Near-identical structures, wildly different activity. I
  care because they break the similarity principle my model
  rests on, and they cap its achievable accuracy — and
  because they are the most useful thing to show a chemist.

"Two molecules, Tanimoto 0.85. Same activity?"
  Probably similar, not necessarily. And 0.85 depends
  entirely on WHICH fingerprint — the number is not
  portable.

"How do you handle mixed IC50 and Ki data?"
  Not by averaging. They measure different things under
  different conditions. Model one endpoint, or model them as
  separate tasks and say which.

"A deployed model started failing. What do you do?"
  Check the INPUTS first: applicability-domain coverage,
  scaffold novelty, whether the assay changed, whether the
  pipeline changed. Suspect the model last.

"When would you NOT use machine learning here?"
  When a rule already works · under ~50 data points · when
  the answer must be mechanistic · when a physics-based
  method is affordable and more accurate.

"Why do you want to work in drug discovery?"
  ⭐ Do not answer with "I find it interesting". Answer with
  something you did because you found it interesting.
Try it · "Walk me through a project where something went wrong." What is the shape of a strong answer?
Answer

Four moves, in this order — and the third is the one candidates skip.

1  THE SITUATION, briefly. What was being decided, and by
   whom. Two sentences, not five minutes of background.

2  WHAT WENT WRONG, stated plainly and owned. Not "the data
   was bad" — "I built the split before I understood that
   the series were analogues, so my validation was
   optimistic."

3  ⭐ HOW YOU FOUND OUT. This is the part that separates a
   real story from a rehearsed one. Did someone tell you?
   Did a prospective result disagree? Did you check?
   Interviewers are listening for whether you have a
   METHOD for discovering your own mistakes, because that
   is what they are actually hiring.

4  WHAT CHANGED AFTERWARDS — in your process, not just in
   that project. "Now I print train/test scaffold overlap
   before I look at any metric."

WHAT SINKS IT
   A story where the mistake was someone else's, or where
   nothing was learned, or where the "failure" was secretly
   a success ("I worked too hard on it"). Pick something
   that genuinely cost you.
Lesson 12

Behavioural rounds, and salary

What behavioural rounds are testing here

Specifically: can you work with chemists? This field's computational people fail most often on communication with the bench, not on technique. Expect questions about explaining a result to a non-specialist, disagreeing with a domain expert, and what you did when your analysis contradicted what someone wanted to hear.

On salary
  • Do not name a number first when you can avoid it; ask for the band.
  • Research the market for the company type and location, not the job title — the same title pays very differently across big pharma, a startup and a CRO.
  • For remote roles, establish early whether pay is location-adjusted. It changes the whole negotiation and it is better known before you are attached to the outcome.
  • Negotiate the whole package: equity, learning budget, conference attendance, compute.
The first 90 days

Learn the data before you propose models. Every organisation's assay history, compound registry and internal conventions are their own dialect, and the fastest credibility you can earn is finding something wrong in their data that they already half-suspected.

Part V

Staying current

Lesson 13

Certifications, honestly

The honest answer

Largely unnecessary in this field. A working repository is stronger evidence than any certificate, because the work is directly inspectable. Do courses for the learning; the certificate at the end is not the point.

When they do help

Three cases, and they are narrow: you are changing fields and need something on the CV to explain the gap · an employer or visa process specifically asks · or the course is genuinely the best way to learn that material and the certificate is incidental.

When they do not

As a substitute for projects. A profile listing six certificates and no repositories reads as someone who has been consuming rather than building, which is the opposite of the signal you want.

And two books, with their limitations stated

Leach & Gillet, An Introduction to Chemoinformatics (2007, revised) is the standard textbook. Representation, searching, descriptors, QSAR, pharmacophores, docking — the fundamentals have not changed. It predates deep learning entirely, so treat it as foundational rather than comprehensive, and pair it with modern tutorials.

Todeschini & Consonni, Molecular Descriptors for Chemoinformatics (2009, two volumes) is an encyclopedia, not a book to read. When a paper uses a descriptor you have never heard of, this tells you what it measures.

Lesson 15

Conferences, journals and people

The one conference to attend

The RDKit UGM — annually, usually October, free or very cheap, with a virtual option that is free and includes all talks. It is the community meeting for open-source cheminformatics, the talks are practical rather than theoretical, and lightning talks are five minutes and genuinely accessible to newcomers. Submitting one is the single cheapest way to become a name in this field.

Then, in order of usefulness

ACS CINF division meetings · the NeurIPS and ICML chemistry workshops (MLDD, AI4Science) for the methods edge · Drug Discovery Chemistry and EFMC-ISMC if you are moving toward the medicinal chemistry side.

Journals and preprints

J. Chem. Inf. Model. and J. Cheminformatics (open access) are the core; J. Med. Chem. for the chemistry. ChemRxiv and arXiv q-bio are where methods appear about a year before the journals, which is the gap worth living in.

Communities

The DeepChem Discord is active and good for questions. RDKit GitHub discussions are not a chat platform but are where maintainers answer directly, and reading them is an education in itself. PyTorch Geometric discussions for GNN work.

Lesson 16

An information diet that survives a full-time job

The firehose problem

More is published every week than you can read in a month, and the failure mode is not missing things — it is skimming so much that nothing changes how you work. Twenty papers skimmed leave no trace. One reimplemented changes your defaults permanently.

A cadence that is actually sustainable
WEEKLY   ~1 hour
   Scan titles from two journal alerts and one preprint feed.
   Save three. Read none yet.

MONTHLY  ~4 hours
   Read ONE paper properly — methods section included — and
   ⭐ REIMPLEMENT THE CORE IDEA on your own data.
   One reimplementation a month beats a paper a day, because
   it produces an artefact and it produces an opinion.

QUARTERLY  ~2 hours
   Review: what did I actually learn? What changed in how I
   work? What is now on the "should know" list that I keep
   postponing?

ANNUALLY
   Attend one conference — the RDKit UGM virtually costs
   nothing. Submit a lightning talk in year two.

⭐ AND THE RULE THAT KEEPS IT HONEST
   If nothing you read this quarter changed a default in
   your own pipeline, you were consuming, not learning.
