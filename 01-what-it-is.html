AI cheminformatics · Volume 01 of 17 · start here

What cheminformatics is. And why anyone pays for it.

A molecule is a physical object. A computer cannot hold one. Everything in this field follows from that single inconvenience — so this first volume is about what the field is trying to do, the problem it exists to serve, and the twenty words you need before the rest of the course will make sense.

Before you start

How to use this course

Fifteen volumes, in order. Each one assumes the volumes before it and nothing else. There is a cheat sheet at the end — Volume 17 — and it is meant to be the thing you keep open once you are working, not something you read now.

Three habits will make the difference between finishing this and abandoning it:

  1. Type the examples. Every worked example in this course is a real string you can paste into a Python session. Reading Descriptors.MolWt(mol) teaches you nothing; running it on a molecule you chose teaches you the whole lesson.
  2. Do the try-it blocks before opening them. They are collapsed for a reason. Getting one wrong and finding out why is the entire mechanism by which this becomes yours.
  3. Accept that you will not understand chemistry deeply, and that this is fine. You need enough chemistry to avoid producing nonsense and to talk to a chemist. You do not need to be one. The course tells you which is which.
What this course is not

It is not a chemistry degree, and it is not a machine-learning course. It assumes you can write Python and have seen a train/test split. Where a machine-learning idea matters — leakage, the choice of metric, what a split really controls for — this course explains the chemical version of it, because that is where people who know ML still get it wrong.

Part I

The field

Three lessons to fix what the word means, what it is next to, and why the computer is involved at all.

Lesson 01

What the word means

Cheminformatics is the use of computers to store, search, and learn from information about molecules. That is the whole definition. The three verbs are worth separating, because they arrived in that order historically and they are still three different jobs:

The three jobs, oldest first
Store
Get a molecule into a computer at all, unambiguously, so that two people writing down the same molecule produce the same thing. This sounds trivial and is not — Volume 03 is entirely about it.
Search
Find molecules. Not by name, but by structure — "everything containing this ring", "everything similar to this one". A chemical database where you can only search by name is almost useless, because the interesting question is always structural.
Learn
Predict something you have not measured. Given 4,000 molecules whose solubility was measured in a lab, predict the solubility of a molecule nobody has made yet. This is the part that has "AI" in front of it, and it is Volumes 08 through 11.

The thing that makes it a distinct field rather than "data science applied to chemistry" is the awkwardness of the object. A molecule is not a row of numbers, not a sentence, and not an image. It is a graph — atoms joined by bonds — with physical rules about which graphs are possible and a three-dimensional shape that the graph does not fully determine. Almost every technique in this course is a different answer to "so how do we turn that into numbers?"

Worked example · why a molecule is not just a formula

Here are two molecules. Both have the molecular formula C₂H₆O.

ETHANOL                        DIMETHYL ETHER
  CCO                            COC
  H H                            H   H
  | |                            |   |
H-C-C-O-H                      H-C-O-C-H
  | |                            |   |
  H H                            H   H

  drinkable                      a gas at room temperature
  boils at 78 °C                 boils at -24 °C

Same atoms. Same counts. Completely different substances. The difference is only in which atom is bonded to which. This is why a molecular formula is not a useful representation, and why the entire field is built on representing connectivity.

Those short strings — CCO and COC — are SMILES, the notation you will use for the rest of the course. Volume 03 teaches you to read them properly. For now just note that they differ, and that the formula does not.

Lesson 02

The three neighbours, and how to tell them apart

Four field names get used loosely and interchangeably in job adverts, which is confusing when you are starting. Here is the honest separation:

FieldWhat it computesWhat it is built onThe tell
Cheminformatics Statistical relationships between structure and property, over many molecules Graph theory, databases, machine learning Works on thousands to millions of molecules at once. Fast and approximate.
Computational chemistry Physical properties from first principles — energies, geometries, reaction barriers Quantum mechanics, physics Works on one molecule at a time, slowly. Minutes to days per molecule.
Bioinformatics Sequences — DNA, RNA, protein String algorithms, statistics, ML The objects are letters in a line, not graphs. Different maths entirely.
Structural bioinformatics The 3D shape of proteins, and what binds to them Geometry, physics, deep learning Where cheminformatics meets biology — Volume 12 lives here.
The one-sentence discriminator

Computational chemistry asks "what does physics say about this molecule?" Cheminformatics asks "what do the other molecules say about this molecule?" One is a simulation; the other is an inference from neighbours. That difference explains their speeds, their failure modes, and why a project usually needs both.

Lesson 03

Why use a computer at all

The honest answer is arithmetic. The number of small organic molecules that could plausibly be drugs is often quoted as around 10⁶⁰ — a figure from a 1996 estimate that is rough by many orders of magnitude but right in the only way that matters: it is unimaginably larger than what anyone can make.

The scale problem, in numbers you can hold
MOLECULES THAT COULD EXIST (drug-like)      ~10^60   (an estimate; treat as "effectively infinite")
MOLECULES EVER SYNTHESISED BY HUMANS        ~10^8    (CAS registry, order of magnitude)
MOLECULES YOU CAN BUY TODAY                 ~10^10   (make-on-demand catalogues, e.g. Enamine REAL)
MOLECULES IN A BIG PUBLIC BIOACTIVITY SET   ~10^6    (ChEMBL, with measured activity)
MOLECULES A LAB CAN TEST PROPERLY PER YEAR  ~10^3-10^5

⭐ THE GAP BETWEEN LINE 1 AND LINE 5 IS THE ENTIRE JUSTIFICATION
   FOR THE FIELD. You cannot test your way through it. You can
   only PREDICT your way through it, then test what the prediction
   says is worth testing.

So the job is triage, not oracle. A model that ranks ten million compounds and puts the good ones near the top is enormously valuable even if it is wrong about most individual compounds. A model that is right 90% of the time about which compounds to ignore saves more money than one that is right 60% of the time about which will succeed. Keep that framing — it comes back in Volume 09 when you meet enrichment factors, and it is the reason chemists judge these models differently from how a data scientist would expect.

The mistake to not make in month one

Do not describe your work as "designing drugs with AI". You are narrowing a search space. Every experienced person in this field will judge how seriously to take you by whether you understand that distinction, because the field has a long history of overclaiming and the people hiring you have been burned by it.

Part II

The problem it serves

You cannot make good decisions about a model without knowing what happens downstream of it. Three lessons on the pipeline your work feeds.

Lesson 04

How a drug actually gets made

Most cheminformatics work exists inside this pipeline, so it is worth knowing even if you end up in materials or agrochemicals instead. Read it as a funnel: each stage costs more per compound than the one before, so the whole game is killing bad compounds as early as possible.

  TARGET IDENTIFICATION          "Which protein, if we blocked it, would treat this disease?"
        |                         Biology. Years. You are usually not here.
        v
  HIT IDENTIFICATION             "Which molecules bind that protein at all?"
        |                         ~10^6 - 10^9 candidates -> a few hundred "hits"
        |                         ** VIRTUAL SCREENING LIVES HERE  (Volume 09) **
        v
  HIT-TO-LEAD                    "Which hits are worth investing in?"
        |                         Confirm they are real; kill the artefacts
        v
  LEAD OPTIMISATION              "Make it more potent, less toxic, better absorbed."
        |                         Hundreds of designed analogues, measured one by one
        |                         ** QSAR AND ADMET MODELS LIVE HERE  (Volumes 08, 09) **
        v
  PRECLINICAL                    Animal safety and pharmacokinetics
        |
        v
  CLINICAL PHASE I / II / III    Humans. Safety, then efficacy, then scale.
        |                         ~90% of what enters Phase I never reaches market
        v
  APPROVAL & MARKET

  TOTAL: commonly cited as 10-15 years and $1-2.6 billion per approved drug
         (the cost figure includes the failures, which is the point)
Why the cost number is so large

The often-quoted ~$2.6 billion is not what one drug costs to make. It is the capitalised cost per success, including every failure along the way, which is why reducing failure rates early is worth so much. If a computational filter kills a compound in month six that would otherwise have failed in Phase II in year nine, it has paid for the entire team. That is the business case for your job, and it is worth being able to state it in one sentence.

Lesson 05

Where you sit in that

Two places, mostly. Both are about narrowing, and neither is about being right.

Position 1 · the funnel top — virtual screening

Someone has a protein target and a catalogue of ten million purchasable compounds. Buying and testing all of them is impossible. Your job is to produce a ranked list of 500 to buy.

You are judged on hit rate: of the 500 you picked, how many turned out active, compared with 500 picked at random. A 20× improvement over random is a good day. Being wrong about 470 of them is normal and expected.

Position 2 · the middle — property and ADMET prediction

A chemist has a promising molecule and twelve ideas for how to modify it. Making and testing all twelve takes six weeks. Your job is to say which three to make first, and to flag any that will fail for a boring reason — too insoluble, metabolised too fast, likely to hit the heart channel that ends careers.

You are judged on whether the chemist's time was well spent, which is a much softer measure and much more about trust than about AUC.

What both have in common

Your output is a decision about what to do next, not a number. A model whose predictions are never acted on has no value regardless of its metrics — and the way you lose the right to be acted on is by being confidently wrong about a compound the chemist knew was silly. Volume 08's applicability domain is the formal version of this worry.

Lesson 06

The economics, and why it shapes the technical choices

Three asymmetries govern almost every methodological decision in this field. They are worth learning now because they explain choices that otherwise look arbitrary.

AsymmetryWhat it meansWhat it causes
False negatives are invisible If your model discards a compound that would have been a blockbuster, nobody ever finds out. A systematic bias towards filters that look safe. It is also why diversity in a selected set is valued — it hedges against a wrong model.
Data is small, expensive and biased A typical target has 200–2,000 measured compounds, produced by chemists who were trying to find actives, not sampling randomly. Random train/test splits lie (Volume 08). Deep learning often loses to a random forest. Public data is dominated by what was publishable.
Testing costs money and time Making one new compound can be weeks of a chemist's work. Precision at the top of the ranking is what matters; overall accuracy mostly does not. And a prediction with an honest uncertainty beats a better point estimate without one.
Try it · A colleague reports a solubility model with R² = 0.91 on a random 20% test split of 1,200 measured compounds. Why should you be sceptical, before seeing anything else?
Answer

Because a random split almost certainly put near-identical molecules on both sides of it. Chemical datasets are built from series — a core structure with twenty small variations around it, made by one chemist over one project. Split those at random and the test set contains compounds whose close siblings are in training. The model can score well by recognising the series rather than learning solubility.

The fix is a scaffold split — group by core structure, keep whole groups together — and R² normally falls a long way when you do it. That drop is not the model getting worse; it is the first honest number you have seen. Volume 08 covers this properly.

Second, smaller point: R² of 0.91 on measured solubility is suspicious on its own, because experimental solubility measurements from different labs often disagree with each other by around half a log unit. A model cannot be meaningfully more consistent than its own labels.

Part III

The vocabulary

Two lessons that do the unglamorous work. Everything after this assumes them.

Lesson 07

Twenty words, defined once

These appear constantly from Volume 02 onwards. You do not need to memorise them — you need to stop being stopped by them. The full 300-term glossary is condensed into Volume 17.

Structures and notation
SMILES
A molecule written as a line of text. CCO is ethanol. The workhorse notation of the field.
SMARTS
SMILES with wildcards — a search pattern for substructures, the way a regular expression is a pattern for text.
InChI
A different string, designed so that one molecule has exactly one possible InChI. Used as a database key; unreadable by humans.
SDF / MOL
A file format that stores atoms, bonds and optionally 3D coordinates, plus arbitrary data fields. The format datasets actually arrive in.
Scaffold
The core ring system of a molecule once you strip the decorations. How chemists group compounds into families.
Turning structures into numbers
Descriptor
A single number computed from a structure — molecular weight, number of rotatable bonds, calculated greasiness.
Fingerprint
A long vector of bits, each recording "is this particular fragment present?". The standard way to make molecules comparable.
Tanimoto similarity
How alike two fingerprints are, from 0 to 1. The field's default notion of "similar molecule". Roughly: shared bits over total bits.
ECFP / Morgan fingerprint
The most widely used fingerprint family. Volume 05 explains how it is built.
Models and their judgement
QSAR
Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship. A model predicting biological activity from structure. The field's bread and butter.
QSPR
The same thing for a physical property rather than biological activity.
Applicability domain
The region of chemical space where a model's predictions can be trusted. Reporting a prediction outside it, without saying so, is the field's cardinal sin.
Activity cliff
Two nearly identical molecules with wildly different activity. The thing that breaks similarity-based reasoning, and the reason models plateau.
Drug discovery words
Target
The protein you are trying to affect.
Ligand
The small molecule that binds to it. In practice, "the drug".
Hit
A compound confirmed to do something to the target, at all. Usually weakly.
Lead
A hit that has survived scrutiny and is worth optimising.
Potency (IC₅₀, Kᵢ, EC₅₀)
How little of the compound you need. Lower is better — this trips up every newcomer. Usually converted to pIC₅₀ = −log₁₀(IC₅₀ in molar), where higher is better.
ADMET
Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, Toxicity — everything that determines whether a potent molecule can actually be a drug. Most failures are here, not in potency.
Docking
Predicting how a ligand sits in a protein's binding pocket, and scoring how well it fits.
Virtual screening
Ranking a large library computationally to choose what to test.
The unit trap that will bite you

IC₅₀ = 10 nM is a better compound than IC₅₀ = 10 µM, by a factor of a thousand. Potency values are concentrations — the amount needed for an effect — so small is good. Worse, public datasets mix nM, µM and M in the same column. Volume 06 shows the curation code for this, and it is the single most common way a beginner's model ends up learning nothing but the unit column.

Lesson 08

The five molecules this course uses

Rather than a new example every time, this course reuses five familiar molecules throughout, so that by Volume 08 you have intuition about them. Learn to recognise these strings.

MoleculeSMILESMWWhy it is in the set
EthanolCCO46.07The smallest thing worth writing down. Good for checking that your code runs at all.
Benzenec1ccccc178.11Introduces aromaticity and the lowercase convention. Six atoms, one ring.
AspirinCC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O180.16A real drug, small enough to read by eye. The course's default example.
ParacetamolCC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1151.16Similar size to aspirin, different chemistry — a natural comparison pair.
CaffeineCn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C194.19Two fused rings and several nitrogens. Where reading SMILES stops being easy.
Worked example · reading aspirin, character by character

You do not need to write SMILES yet. You need to stop finding them opaque.

CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O

C          a carbon atom
C(=O)      a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen      -- this is a C=O group
O          an oxygen, joining that group to what follows
c1ccccc1   six aromatic carbons forming a ring      -- lowercase means aromatic
                                                       the 1s mark where the ring closes
C(=O)O     another C=O, this one also carrying an -OH  -- a carboxylic acid

READ ALOUD: "an acetyl group, attached through an oxygen to a benzene ring,
             which also carries a carboxylic acid"
             ...which is the chemical name: acetylsalicylic acid.

Three rules explain most of what you just read: letters are atoms; brackets are branches hanging off the main chain; a digit marks a ring opening and the matching digit closes it. Hydrogens are not written — they are assumed to fill whatever is left over.

Try it · Which of these two is paracetamol, and how can you tell without knowing any chemistry? (a) CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1 (b) CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O
Answer

(a). Two tells, both purely structural:

  • (b) contains C(=O)O at the end — a carboxylic acid — and (a) does not. From the table above, the acid is aspirin's distinguishing feature.
  • (a) contains an N. Paracetamol has a nitrogen; aspirin has none. Counting element letters is often enough to tell two molecules apart.

Also worth noticing: in (a) the ring is written c1ccc(O)cc1 — the (O) sits inside the ring's character run, meaning an OH hangs off that ring atom. Branches in brackets can appear anywhere, including mid-ring.

Part IV

The work

Lesson 09

The roles, and which one you are aiming at

TitleWhat they actually doWhat they are hired for
Cheminformatician / Computational chemistBuilds models, runs screens, supports project teams of medicinal chemists.Chemistry judgement first, coding second. Usually needs real chemistry background.
ML scientist, drug discoveryBuilds and trains the models — GNNs, generative models, foundation models.ML depth first. Chemistry learned on the job, and the good ones learn it fast.
Scientific software engineerBuilds the platforms the scientists use — pipelines, data layers, internal tools.Software engineering, with enough domain fluency to model the data correctly.
Data engineer, chemistry dataIngests, normalises and serves compound and assay data at scale.Data engineering plus an unusual tolerance for messy scientific units.
If you are arriving from software rather than chemistry

The third and fourth rows are the realistic entry points, and they are not a consolation prize — chemistry organisations are chronically short of people who can build reliable software, and the data problems are genuinely hard. The route in is to be the person who can do both halves of a conversation: enough chemistry to know why the units matter, enough engineering to make the pipeline that fixes them. Volume 16 covers the portfolio that demonstrates this.

Lesson 10

What the work actually looks like

A realistic composite week, because the job is less glamorous and more interesting than the papers suggest:

  MON   A chemist asks: "can you predict solubility for these 40 ideas?"
        You check whether the 40 fall inside your model's applicability domain.
        Eleven do not. You say so. This is the most valuable thing you do all week.

  TUE   Assay data arrives from a partner. Three different units, two molecules
        drawn as different tautomers of the same thing, one SMILES that will not
        parse. You spend the day on curation.        <-- Volume 06

  WED   Retrain. The new model is slightly worse on the scaffold split and slightly
        better on the random split. You go with the scaffold number and explain why.

  THU   A generative run produced 5,000 "novel" molecules. 4,100 are unsynthesisable
        or silly. You build a filter cascade and hand over 60.   <-- Volumes 09, 11

  FRI   You write down what the model does NOT cover, and put it in the README.
The pattern in that week

Four of the five days are data curation, honest evaluation, and saying what you do not know. Roughly one is modelling. That ratio is not a sign of an immature team — it is the job. The volumes in this course are weighted the same way on purpose.
