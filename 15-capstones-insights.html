Before you start

What a capstone is for

The claim

One finished capstone with a deployed demo and an honest evaluation outperforms all nine exercises in a hiring conversation. The exercises show you can do the steps; the capstone is the only artefact that shows judgement sustained over time — what you chose to build, what you chose to leave out, and whether you can say where it fails.

Capstone 1 · 15–20 hours

An end-to-end QSAR platform

The centrepiece project of the course: raw bioactivity data through to a served, containerised prediction service with an interface a chemist can use.

  THE FIFTEEN COMPONENTS

  DATA        1  acquisition — API query, filters, raw data saved
              2  curation — full pipeline + a quality report
              3  features — two feature types, variance and NaN checks

  MODELS      4  random forest baseline, tuned
              5  XGBoost, tuned
              6  a Chemprop GNN, trained on the scaffold split
              7  comparison — one table, five metrics, all three models

  HONESTY     8  scaffold split, implemented correctly
              9  ⭐ applicability domain, visualised
             10  ⭐ uncertainty — conformal prediction with a
                 coverage check

  DELIVERY   11  FastAPI service with input validation
             12  Streamlit dashboard, four views
             13  Docker — `docker compose up` must work
             14  documentation — README, docstrings, architecture
             15  code quality — modular, not one notebook

  ⭐ NOTE WHAT MAKES THIS DIFFERENT FROM A TUTORIAL PROJECT:
     components 8, 9 and 10. Anyone can train three models. The
     scaffold split, the applicability domain and the calibrated
     uncertainty are what say you understand what a prediction
     is worth.
Capstone 1

How it is scored — and what the rubric is really telling you

ComponentWeightMeets the standardExceptional
Data curation10%Full pipeline with a quality reportPlus activity-cliff analysis and a scaffold-diversity report
Feature engineering10%Two feature types, variance and NaN checksFeature-importance analysis and a manifest file
Applicability domain8%One method, visualisedTwo methods, a Williams plot, and an AD-versus-error correlation
Uncertainty8%Conformal prediction with a coverage checkAdaptive intervals and a calibration curve
Scaffold split5%Implemented correctlyMultiple splits analysed, scaffold difficulty studied
Deployment21%Working API, four dashboard views, docker compose upTests, logging, rate limiting, health checks, multi-stage build
Documentation and code9%README, docstrings, modular codeMethods and results sections, a reproducibility guide, type hints, CI
Read the weights, not the components

Curation and features together are worth 20% — more than any single model, and more than all three models' hyperparameter tuning combined. The AD and uncertainty components are worth 16%, roughly double the GNN. The rubric is making the same argument the whole course has been making: the modelling is the easy part, and what distinguishes competent work is the data going in and the honesty coming out.

Passing is 45 of 75 with a minimum of 3 on every component — so you cannot compensate for a missing applicability domain with an excellent dashboard. That constraint is also deliberate.

Try it · You have 20 hours. Where do you spend the last four — tuning the GNN, or building the dashboard?
Answer

The dashboard, almost always — but for a reason that is not about the rubric.

By weight it is close: the GNN is 8%, the dashboard 8%. But a tuned GNN and an untuned GNN look identical to anyone reading your repository, while a working dashboard is the only part of this project a non-specialist can see at all. A hiring manager who opens your README sees a screenshot or sees a wall of text.

There is also a diminishing-returns argument: if your GNN is already training and evaluating on the scaffold split, four more hours of tuning will move a metric by a few percent — and that few percent is well inside the uncertainty you are supposed to be reporting. You would be spending four hours on a difference you have already argued is not significant.

The exception: if the GNN currently fails to train, fix it. A component scoring 1 caps your whole submission, and "it did not work" is the one thing that cannot be written up honestly.

Capstone 2 · 12–15 hours

A virtual screening campaign

The build

Pick a target, prepare the structure, curate a library, dock it, rescore the hits with ML, filter for ADMET, triage, and report. The deliverable is written as a campaign report, not a notebook: executive summary, target description, protein preparation, library, docking, rescoring, filtering, hit triage.

The step that proves you know what you are doing

Retrospective validation before the prospective run. Known actives plus property-matched decoys, with enrichment at 1% reported. Without it the campaign is an opinion; with it, it is a method. This single step separates most candidates.

What juniors submit

A ranked list of docking scores presented as affinities, with no funnel, no enrichment number, and no acknowledgement that the top 100 are the same molecule in different coats.

The artefact

The funnel with counts at every stage, the enrichment figure, and twenty compounds each with a pose image and a one-line rationale. Plus the sentence that earns trust: "docking scores carry 2–3 kcal/mol of error, so these are ranked candidates, not predicted affinities."

Capstone 3 · 10–12 hours

A molecular generator

The build

Train a generative model, define a multi-objective scoring function, generate thousands of candidates, filter and evaluate them, analyse the chemical space and the SAR, and assess synthetic feasibility.

The two things that must be in it
  • Synthesizability inside the scoring function, not only as a post-filter — the difference between a pipeline that works and one that discards 98% of its output at the last step.
  • Uniqueness and scaffold count plotted against reward, so mode collapse is visible instead of hidden by a rising score.
The honest finish

Report the whole funnel: how many generated, how many survived each filter, how many had a solved retrosynthetic route. "100,000 generated, 60 with routes" is a far more credible result than "we generated 100,000 novel molecules" — and it is the same work, described truthfully. Capstone 3 has an explicit Limitations section in its report template, which is itself a lesson.

Choosing a target

The choice that decides whether the project is finishable
WHAT MAKES A GOOD CAPSTONE TARGET

  ✓ ENOUGH DATA — roughly 1,000+ measured compounds in ChEMBL
    with a consistent assay. Below ~500 you spend the project
    fighting the dataset instead of building the platform.

  ✓ SCAFFOLD DIVERSITY — several distinct chemical series.
    ⭐ A target with one series cannot demonstrate a scaffold
       split, which removes the most important thing you were
       going to show.

  ✓ A STRUCTURE, if you are doing Capstone 2 — ideally a
    co-crystal with a ligand bound, not just an apo form.

  ✓ NOT OVERDONE — a target with 400 published models means
    your work is compared against all of them. Something
    slightly less famous reads as your own choice.

  AND THE PRACTICAL ONE

  ✓ PICK ONE TARGET FOR ALL THREE CAPSTONES.
    The curation from Capstone 1 feeds Capstone 2's library
    work and Capstone 3's training set. Three projects on one
    target is a coherent portfolio with a narrative; three
    projects on three targets is three times the data work
    and reads as three unrelated exercises.
Part II

Seven field insights

The field rests on a handful of ideas. Each of these is given here as the one sentence that reframes it, then what that reframing changes, then the misunderstanding that trips people up.

F01

Lipinski's rule of five

The one idea

Lipinski's rule of five is not about chemistry. It is about statistics.

What that reframing changes

It describes where the probability of oral bioavailability is highest, based on historical data. There is no physical law saying a molecule with MW 501 cannot be absorbed — 500 is an empirical boundary drawn across a continuous distribution. So a compound at MW 505 has not "failed" anything; it has landed slightly further down a smooth curve.

The misunderstanding

Treating it as a filter in a WHERE clause. Many important drugs violate it — most natural products, and essentially all "beyond rule of five" chemistry. Used as a delete, it removes the interesting compounds first.

How to use it

As a flag that starts a conversation: "three of these violate Ro5 — is oral dosing actually a requirement here?" For an injectable, or a topical, the whole rule may be irrelevant.

F02

The molecular similarity principle

The one idea

Molecular similarity is not a property of molecules. It is a property of the representation you choose.

What that reframing changes

Two molecules do not have a similarity. They have a similarity given a fingerprint, a metric and a context. Change the fingerprint and it changes; change Tanimoto for Dice and it changes; change the biological question from kinase inhibition to metabolic stability and what counts as similar changes completely.

The misunderstanding

Quoting "Tanimoto 0.85" as though it were a measured physical quantity. It is a number produced by a choice you made, and it is not portable between fingerprint types or sizes.

The practical consequence

Use more than one fingerprint when the answer matters, and always name which one produced the number. And expect similarity methods to be good at triage across diverse libraries and poor at fine discrimination within a series — which is unfortunate, because the second is what medicinal chemists want most.

F03

Applicability domain

The one idea

A prediction without a confidence boundary is not a prediction. It is a guess. Described in the source as the single most important mindset shift in computational chemistry, and that is not an overstatement.

What that reframing changes 
When you report "predicted IC50 = 50 nM", you are implying a
certainty you do not have. That prediction might be off by

    2-fold     acceptable
    10-fold    problematic
    1000-fold  catastrophic

⭐ AND WITHOUT AN APPLICABILITY DOMAIN, YOU HAVE NO IDEA
   WHICH OF THOSE THREE YOU ARE IN.
Why it keeps being omitted

Because the model does not complain. It returns a confident-looking number for chemistry it has never seen, and nothing in the output distinguishes an interpolation from an extrapolation. The compounds you most want to predict are new — which means they sit closest to the edge.

The habit

Every prediction ships with a domain flag. Every performance claim is reported inside and outside the domain separately. If those two numbers are the same, your domain definition is not doing anything and you should fix it rather than report it.

F04

Activity cliffs

The one idea

Activity cliffs are not problems to be solved. They are the most valuable information in your dataset — and your workflow should be designed to find them, understand them and communicate them.

What that reframing changes

The instinct is to treat a cliff as noise that hurts your R². The reversal: a cliff is a place where a specific interaction is being made or lost, which is exactly the mechanistic information a medicinal chemist can act on. Your model's error on cliffs is the price of the insight, not a defect to be smoothed away.

The misunderstanding

Reporting cliffs without checking assay variability. A 3-fold difference inside 2-fold assay error is not a cliff, and a chemist who synthesises compounds based on it has wasted weeks.

The consequence for your models

Cliffs cap the accuracy any similarity-based method can reach, because the two sides of a cliff look nearly identical to a fingerprint. A model reporting high R² on a cliff-rich dataset is averaging over precisely the cases people care about.

F05

The AlphaFold revolution

The one idea

AlphaFold democratised structural biology, but it did not democratise drug design. The bottleneck was never the structure — it was always the chemistry.

What that reframing changes

The pre-AlphaFold narrative was "if only we had more structures, we could design drugs". We now have structures for essentially every protein, and the number of drugs did not jump. Knowing the shape of the lock does not tell you how to make the key, and it certainly does not tell you how tightly the key will be held.

The misunderstanding

Treating a predicted structure as equivalent to an experimental one. AlphaFold predicts the apo form; induced fit, binding-site rotamers, loop conformations and allosteric states are not reliably captured. And a structure is not an affinity — docking into a perfect model still carries docking's 2–3 kcal/mol error.

The practical rule

Read pLDDT for the binding-site residues rather than the global average, and prefer an experimental holo structure over a perfect prediction whenever one exists.

F06

Free energy perturbation

The one idea

FEP is not just a prediction tool — it is a thinking tool. It forces you to reason about binding in terms of thermodynamic cycles, and that habit makes you better regardless of which method you actually run.

What the cycle teaches

Absolute binding free energy is very hard; relative free energy between two similar ligands is tractable. The move is to avoid the hard quantity entirely by constructing a cycle whose endpoints are what you care about. That pattern — replace an intractable absolute question with a tractable relative one — recurs far beyond FEP.

The numbers

R² around 0.5–0.7 for ranking, against docking's 0.1–0.3. Hours to days per molecule. A validated binding mode required. It is a tens-of-compounds method, and anyone proposing it on a screening library has misunderstood what it is.

Where it is heading

Machine-learned potentials are starting to replace parts of the force-field evaluation, which attacks the cost directly. Worth watching; not yet worth assuming.

F07

Chemical language models

The one idea

The real power is not that they treat molecules as text. It is that they enable transfer learning — which converts the entire corpus of known chemistry into prior knowledge for your specific task.

What that reframing changes

It stops the argument about whether SMILES is a "good" representation, which is the wrong question. SMILES is a mediocre representation that happens to unlock pretraining on hundreds of millions of unlabelled molecules — and for a project with 200 labelled compounds, that trade is overwhelmingly worth making.

The limit that does not go away

A string is not a molecule. SMILES is non-unique without canonicalisation, non-local — atoms bonded in the molecule can sit far apart in the string — and carries no 3D information. Anything requiring geometry is invisible to a model reading strings.

The practical consequence

Excellent at pattern completion over chemical text; unreliable at geometry and arithmetic. Which is why the rule from Volume 13 holds: the model decides what to compute, RDKit computes it.
