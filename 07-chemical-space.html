Part I

Chemical space

Lesson 01

What "chemical space" means

Chemical space is the space whose axes are molecular properties and whose points are molecules. If you describe each molecule by 2,048 fingerprint bits, your chemical space has 2,048 dimensions and each molecule is one point in it.

The phrase is used loosely for three different things, and separating them prevents confusion:

The space of all possible molecules

The ~10⁶⁰ figure from Volume 01. Conceptual, unenumerable, used rhetorically.

The space your data occupies

The actual cloud of points your 1,173 compounds form. This is what you can plot, and what determines your applicability domain.

The space a projection shows

A 2D picture made from the above by discarding 2,046 dimensions. Useful and deeply misleading in equal measure.

Lesson 02

PCA, t-SNE and UMAP — what each actually does

MethodTries to preserveSo it is good forAnd it lies about
PCA Global variance — the directions in which the data spreads most Honest overall shape; distances between far-apart groups mean something Local detail. On sparse fingerprints the first two components often explain very little of the variance — check, and report the figure.
t-SNE Local neighbourhoods — who is near whom Revealing clusters that exist in the data Everything global. Distances between clusters are meaningless, cluster sizes are meaningless, and the layout changes completely with a different random seed or perplexity.
UMAP Local neighbourhoods, with somewhat better global structure The usual practical default — faster than t-SNE and more stable Still substantially. Between-cluster distance is more meaningful than in t-SNE but is not a real distance.
Worked example · projecting a library 
import numpy as np, umap
from rdkit.Chem import rdFingerprintGenerator

gen = rdFingerprintGenerator.GetMorganGenerator(radius=2, fpSize=2048)
X = np.array([gen.GetFingerprintAsNumPy(m) for m in mols])     # (n, 2048)

reducer = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1,
                    metric="jaccard",        # <-- NOT euclidean
                    random_state=42)         # <-- so the picture is reproducible
coords = reducer.fit_transform(X)

plt.scatter(coords[:,0], coords[:,1], c=df["pIC50"], cmap="viridis", s=8)

Two arguments there are the whole lesson:

  • metric="jaccard" — Jaccard is Tanimoto, for binary vectors. Euclidean distance on sparse bit vectors is close to meaningless, because almost every pair is nearly equidistant. Using the default silently produces a picture of nothing.
  • random_state=42 — without it, the same data gives a different picture every run, and you will draw conclusions from an arrangement that is not stable.
Lesson 03

How to read the plot — and what not to conclude

Four conclusions people draw that the plot does not support
  • "These two clusters are far apart, so they are very different." In t-SNE and UMAP, between-cluster distance is an artefact of the layout algorithm. Not a fact about chemistry.
  • "This cluster is bigger, so it has more chemical diversity." Cluster area in t-SNE carries no information at all.
  • "There are four clusters." There are four blobs in this projection with these parameters. Change n_neighbors and you may get seven. Verify with an actual clustering algorithm in the full space — Lesson 04.
  • "The actives are all in one region, so the model will be easy." Possibly — or the projection collapsed the dimension that separates them. Check in the original space.
What the plot IS good for

Three legitimate uses. Seeing whether your test set overlaps your training set (colour by split — if test points sit entirely inside the training cloud, your split is too easy). Seeing whether a new library covers the same region as your model's training data. And spotting an obviously separate group that turns out to be a data problem — a batch of fragments, or a set of compounds from a different source. Use it to generate questions, never to answer them.

Part II

Grouping molecules

Lesson 04

Butina clustering — the field's default

k-means needs a number of clusters and a mean, and neither makes much sense for bit vectors. Butina clustering is the standard alternative: it works directly from a similarity threshold and decides the number of clusters itself.

Worked example · clustering, and what the counts tell you 
from rdkit import DataStructs
from rdkit.ML.Cluster import Butina

fps = [gen.GetFingerprint(m) for m in mols]

# Butina wants a flat lower-triangular DISTANCE list (1 - similarity)
dists = []
for i in range(1, len(fps)):
    sims = DataStructs.BulkTanimotoSimilarity(fps[i], fps[:i])
    dists.extend(1 - s for s in sims)

clusters = Butina.ClusterData(dists, len(fps), 0.4,   # 0.4 distance = 0.6 similarity
                              isDistData=True)

print(f"{len(clusters)} clusters from {len(fps)} compounds")
print("largest:", len(clusters[0]))
singletons = sum(1 for c in clusters if len(c) == 1)
print(f"{singletons} singletons")

Read the three numbers together — they characterise your dataset:

  • 1,173 compounds → 40 clusters means you have roughly 40 distinct chemical ideas, not 1,173. Your effective sample size for learning general structure–activity relationships is closer to 40.
  • A largest cluster of 400 means a third of your data is one series, and a random split will be dominated by it.
  • Many singletons means many compounds have no close neighbour — those are the ones a similarity-based model will predict worst, and they are worth identifying now.

Butina is O(n²) in memory for the distance list, so above roughly 50,000 compounds you need a sparser approach — or cluster a random subsample and assign the rest to the nearest centroid.

Lesson 05

Scaffold analysis — the diversity number to report

Worked example · how diverse is this dataset really? 
from rdkit.Chem.Scaffolds import MurckoScaffold
from collections import Counter

scaffolds = Counter(
    Chem.MolToSmiles(MurckoScaffold.GetScaffoldForMol(m)) for m in mols
)

print(f"{len(mols)} compounds, {len(scaffolds)} unique scaffolds")
for scaf, n in scaffolds.most_common(5):
    print(f"  {n:4d}  {scaf}")

  1173 compounds, 212 unique scaffolds
    287  c1ccc(-c2ccncc2)cc1          <-- one quarter of the set
    141  O=C(Nc1ccccc1)c1ccccc1
     98  c1ccccc1
     ...
  singleton scaffolds: 108

The compound-to-scaffold ratio is the honest diversity measure, and 1,173 : 212 is a normal, series-heavy medicinal chemistry dataset. Quote it whenever you quote a dataset size, because "1,173 compounds" and "212 scaffolds" describe very different amounts of information.

The 108 singleton scaffolds matter for a different reason: in a scaffold split they can only ever be in one fold, so they are the compounds most likely to be predicted badly and most important to report separately.

Lesson 06

Diversity selection — picking n compounds that are not all the same

A recurring real task: "we can afford to buy 500 compounds from this list of 200,000 — pick them." Taking the top 500 by model score gives you 500 near-identical analogues, which is a bad bet when the model might be wrong.

MaxMin — the standard algorithm

Pick a compound. Then repeatedly pick the compound whose minimum distance to everything already picked is largest. Greedy, simple, and it spreads the selection out.

from rdkit.SimDivFilters.rdSimDivPickers import MaxMinPicker
picker = MaxMinPicker()
idx = picker.LazyBitVectorPick(fps, len(fps), 500)   # 500 diverse indices
The tension you are managing

Score says "buy the best". Diversity says "hedge". Pure score risks all 500 on one wrong hypothesis; pure diversity ignores everything the model learned.

What is actually done

Cluster the top-scoring compounds, then take the best few from each cluster. Take the top 5,000 by score, cluster them, pick the top 2–3 from each of 200 clusters. You keep the model's signal and you are not betting everything on one series. This "cluster-then-pick" pattern is the standard answer and it is what Volume 09 uses.

Seeing structure–activity

Lesson 07

The activity landscape

Plot structural similarity against activity difference for every pair of compounds, and the shape of the resulting cloud tells you how learnable your dataset is:

   activity
   difference
      ^
  3.0 |                                  .
      |   C  C                        .     .
  2.0 |  C C  C                    .  .  .     .
      |                         .   .  .   .  .
  1.0 |                     .  . .  . .  .  .
      |                  . . .. ... . .. . .
  0.0 +--------------------------------------------> similarity
      0.0                                        1.0

      REGION "C" (top right)  = HIGH similarity, HIGH activity difference
                              = ACTIVITY CLIFFS
                              = where models fail, and where the
                                interesting chemistry is

      The broad diagonal cloud = the similarity principle working:
                                 more similar -> more similar activity
Why this plot is worth making early

It sets your expectations before you train anything. A dataset dense with cliffs will not support a high R², no matter which algorithm you use — and knowing that on day two is much better than concluding "the model is bad" on day thirty. It also gives you a specific list of compound pairs to show a chemist, who will often explain the cliff immediately.

Lesson 08

Matched molecular pairs

A matched molecular pair (MMP) is two molecules differing by one small, well-defined change — a hydrogen becomes a methyl, a chlorine becomes a fluorine. Because only one thing changed, the difference in activity is attributable to that change.

Worked example · what an MMP table gives you 
TRANSFORMATION          n pairs   median ΔpIC50   interpretation
  H -> F                    47        +0.3        usually mildly helpful
  H -> CH3                  82        +0.5        the "magic methyl" effect
  H -> OH                   31        -0.2        usually neutral or harmful here
  OCH3 -> OH                19        -1.1        strongly harmful in this series
  Cl -> CF3                 23        +0.8        consistently helpful

⭐ THIS TABLE IS SOMETHING A CHEMIST CAN ACT ON TOMORROW.
   It does not require a model, it does not require trust in a
   black box, and it says "try a CF3 where you currently have Cl".

MMP analysis is often more useful to a project than a QSAR model, and it is much easier to get right. It is also the most interpretable way to find activity cliffs: sort the pairs by absolute ΔpIC₅₀ and the top of that list is your cliff set, already explained in terms of a specific chemical change.

The standard tool is mmpdb. The underlying idea — fragment every molecule at single bonds, index by the constant part, group by what differs — is simple enough to implement yourself for a small dataset.

Making it a pipeline

Lesson 09

From notebook to module

The shape that survives reuse
src/
  data.py        load_chembl(target_id)  ->  raw DataFrame
  curate.py      standardise(smiles)     ->  str | None
                 curate(df)              ->  (clean_df, report_dict)
  features.py    featurise(mols, kind)   ->  np.ndarray
  splits.py      scaffold_split(df, frac)->  (train_idx, test_idx)
  models.py      train(X, y)             ->  model, metrics

TWO RULES THAT MAKE THE DIFFERENCE:

1. EVERY FUNCTION TAKES AND RETURNS PLAIN DATA.
   Not "a function that also writes a file and prints a plot".
   Pure functions are testable; procedures are not.

2. THE CURATION FUNCTION RETURNS ITS REPORT.
   curate(df) -> (clean_df, report)
   The counts from Volume 06 are a RETURN VALUE, not a print
   statement — so the pipeline can write them to a file and
   the test can assert on them.
Lesson 10

Testing chemical code

Chemical code has a property that makes testing unusually valuable: it fails silently. A wrong fingerprint parameter does not raise — it produces a slightly worse model you cannot explain. These are the tests that catch that class of bug.

Worked example · four tests worth writing 
def test_standardise_removes_salt():
    assert standardise("CC(=O)[O-].[Na+]") == "CC(=O)O"
    # explicit known input -> known output. The whole point.

def test_standardise_returns_none_on_garbage():
    assert standardise("not a molecule") is None
    # the failure path is a feature and must be tested

def test_canonicalisation_is_idempotent():
    once  = standardise("OCC")
    twice = standardise(once)
    assert once == twice
    # running the pipeline twice must not change anything.
    # this catches a whole class of ordering bugs.

def test_scaffold_split_shares_no_scaffold():
    train, test = scaffold_split(df, 0.8)
    assert not (scaffolds_of(train) & scaffolds_of(test))
    # THE MOST IMPORTANT TEST IN THE REPOSITORY.
    # If this passes silently when broken, every number you report is wrong.

The fourth test is the one to write first. A scaffold split that is quietly not splitting by scaffold produces optimistic numbers that look plausible, survive review, and are wrong — the exact profile of a bug that does real damage. Assert the invariant, not the implementation.

One more habit: the golden dataset

Keep 20 molecules with known, hand-checked descriptor values in the repository, and assert your pipeline reproduces them. When you upgrade RDKit, this test tells you whether anything changed underneath you — which, per Volume 02, it occasionally does.

Try it · Your UMAP plot shows two clean, well-separated clusters. A colleague says "so there are two chemical classes here". What is wrong with that?
Answer

A UMAP plot cannot support that conclusion, and three separate things could be producing it.

1  UMAP PRESERVES LOCAL STRUCTURE, NOT GLOBAL STRUCTURE.
   The distance BETWEEN two clusters is not meaningful.
   Neither is their size, nor the empty space separating
   them. Only "these points are near each other" survives.

2  THE SEPARATION MAY BE A PARAMETER.
   n_neighbors and min_dist change the number of visible
   clusters dramatically. Re-run with different settings —
   if the two clusters merge, they were a setting.

3  IT MAY BE ENCODING SOMETHING TRIVIAL.
   Colour the plot by molecular weight, by heavy-atom count,
   by source dataset. Very often the "two chemical classes"
   are two SUPPLIERS, or two size regimes.

What to do instead: cluster in the original fingerprint space — Butina, say — and use the projection only to display those clusters. Then the claim rests on the real distances rather than on a 2D picture of them.

The general rule worth carrying: a projection is a way to look at a dataset, never a way to conclude something about it.

Lesson 11

The visual pipeline tools, and when they win

What KNIME is

A visual workflow tool: you assemble a pipeline by wiring nodes together on a canvas rather than writing a script. It has a mature cheminformatics extension, and it is genuinely widespread in pharma — you will meet it, so it is worth knowing what it is for rather than dismissing it.

Where it genuinely wins
  • Chemists build their own workflows in it. That is the whole case, and it is a strong one — a pipeline its user can modify beats a better pipeline they must queue for.
  • The data flow is visible, which makes a workflow easy to explain in a meeting.
  • Good connector coverage for the commercial tools a pharma company already owns.
Where it loses

Version control, code review, testing and reproducibility — all much weaker than a Python repository. A large KNIME workflow is difficult to diff and difficult to hand over.

The judgement

Prototype and hand-off work in KNIME; production pipelines in code. And do not be snobbish about it in an interview — saying "KNIME is for people who cannot code" is both wrong and a good way to insult half the room.
