AI cheminformatics · Volume 09 of 17 · phase 2

Virtual screening and ADMET. Narrowing millions, then killing the survivors early.

Two halves of the same discipline. Screening takes a library too large to test and produces a list you can afford to buy. ADMET takes a potent molecule and asks whether it could ever be a drug — which is where most candidates actually die, long after potency stopped being the problem.

Part I

Virtual screening

Lesson 01

The two families

ApproachNeedsAsksUse when
Ligand-based Known active molecules "What else looks like these?" You have actives but no protein structure. Fast, robust, the workhorse.
Structure-based A 3D protein structure "What fits in this pocket?" You have a structure and few or no known actives. Slower, more assumptions, can find genuinely novel chemistry. Volume 12.
The practical answer is usually "both"

Use ligand-based to rank fast, structure-based to check the survivors. Docking two million compounds is expensive; docking the twenty thousand that a similarity search already liked is cheap, and the two methods fail differently — so agreement between them is genuine evidence in a way that either alone is not.

Lesson 02

The cascade — and why the order is forced

  2,000,000  purchasable compounds
       |
       |  1. PROPERTY FILTERS          milliseconds each
       |     lead-like ranges, element whitelist, size bounds
       v
  1,200,000
       |
       |  2. STRUCTURAL ALERTS         milliseconds
       |     PAINS, reactive groups, unwanted substructures
       v
    950,000
       |
       |  3. LIGAND-BASED RANKING      microseconds each, in bulk
       |     similarity to knowns, or a trained QSAR model
       v
     20,000    (top 2%)
       |
       |  4. DOCKING                   seconds to minutes EACH
       |     -- this is why it comes fourth, not first
       v
      2,000
       |
       |  5. CLUSTER, THEN PICK        Volume 07, Lesson 06
       |     + a human chemist looking at every one
       v
        500  compounds to buy and test

  ⭐ THE ORDERING RULE: CHEAPEST FILTER FIRST, ALWAYS.
     Docking 2M compounds at 10 seconds each is 231 CPU-days.
     Docking 20,000 is 2.3 days. The cascade exists to make
     the expensive step affordable — and every stage must
     therefore be cheaper than the one after it.
The cost of a cascade: errors compound downward

A compound wrongly removed at stage 1 can never be recovered at stage 5. The cascade multiplies your false-negative rate at every stage, and — per Volume 01 — you will never find out. So early filters must be loose and defensible, and the aggressive discrimination belongs late, where you still have the option of looking at what you are discarding.

Lesson 03

Preparing the library

A vendor catalogue is not ready to screen. The preparation is Volume 06's curation with three additions:

  1. Standardise — desalt, neutralise, canonical tautomer. Exactly as before.
  2. Enumerate what is ambiguous. A compound supplied with unspecified stereochemistry is really several compounds; a compound with an ionisable group exists as several protonation states at physiological pH. For docking, the state you choose changes the result, so generate the plausible states rather than silently picking one.
  3. Generate conformers, if docking. Per Volume 03, a flexible molecule has no single shape. Most docking programs handle flexibility internally; shape-based methods need an explicit conformer ensemble.
The number that surprises people

Library preparation can multiply your compound count several-fold. Two stereocentres unspecified plus two ionisable groups can turn one catalogue entry into a dozen structures to evaluate. Budget for it, and keep the mapping back to the original catalogue ID — because at the end you have to order something, and the vendor sells the entry, not your enumerated variant.

Lesson 04

Ligand-based methods

MethodHow it ranksStrength and weakness
Similarity searchTanimoto to one or more known activesTrivial, fast, effective. Finds analogues — so it will not find new scaffolds.
Similarity ensembleMaximum or mean similarity across all known activesBetter coverage than a single query. Max is usually preferred; mean dilutes a strong match.
Trained QSAR modelPredicted activity from Volume 08Uses inactives too, so it learns what not to pick. Watch the applicability domain — most of a 2M library is outside it.
PharmacophoreMatching a 3D arrangement of features — donor here, ring there, at these distancesCan find new scaffolds, because it abstracts away the chemistry and keeps the geometry. Needs conformers and careful model building.
Shape similarity3D volume overlap with a known activeAlso scaffold-hopping. Conformer-dependent.
Worked example · similarity screening at scale 
from rdkit import DataStructs

# known actives -> fingerprints
query_fps = [gen.GetFingerprint(m) for m in known_actives]

# score = the BEST similarity to ANY known active
scores = []
for fp in library_fps:                       # 2,000,000 of them
    best = max(DataStructs.BulkTanimotoSimilarity(fp, query_fps))
    scores.append(best)

top = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:20000]       # top 1%

Two practical notes. Use BulkTanimotoSimilarity — the loop runs in C++ and the Python version is roughly a hundred times slower, which is the difference between an hour and a week. And store the library's fingerprints once, on disk, rather than recomputing them for every campaign.

One conceptual note. Taking the maximum similarity means one very similar active is enough. That is usually right for finding analogues and is exactly why this method will not give you a new scaffold — it is structurally incapable of it.

Lesson 05

Measuring enrichment — the metric that convinces people

Worked example · enrichment, worked through 
SETUP
  library            100,000 compounds
  actives in it        1,000  (1% — the base rate)
  you test the top     1,000  (1%) of your ranking
  actives found          150

ENRICHMENT FACTOR AT 1%

  hit rate in your selection   = 150 / 1,000   = 15%
  hit rate at random           = 1,000/100,000 =  1%

  EF(1%) = 15% / 1% = 15x

WHAT THAT IS WORTH, IN THE LANGUAGE OF A BUDGET

  to find 150 actives at random you would test 15,000 compounds
  you tested 1,000
  at ~$50 per compound tested, that is $700,000 saved

⭐ THIS IS THE SENTENCE THAT JUSTIFIES YOUR JOB. Learn to say it.
   "15-fold enrichment" means nothing to a director; "we found the
   same number of hits for one fifteenth of the cost" does.

Typical numbers, so you can calibrate: 10–50× for ligand-based screening with good known actives; 2–10× for docking against a decent structure; 100×+ is excellent and worth double-checking for a leak or an easy benchmark.

Two ways enrichment numbers get inflated

Analogue bias. If your test actives are close analogues of your query actives, a similarity search finds them trivially and the enrichment means nothing about prospective performance. Benchmark on actives from a different series.

Decoy bias. Benchmark sets like DUD-E pair actives with property-matched "decoys" that are assumed inactive. Known analyses have shown such sets carry biases a model can exploit — learning to tell actives from decoys rather than actives from inactives. Good scores on these benchmarks transfer to reality less well than they appear to.

Lesson 06

Triage — choosing what to actually buy

The score is not the selection

Taking the top 500 by score gives you 500 near-identical compounds and one hypothesis. If the hypothesis is wrong, you learn nothing from any of them.

The standard procedure
  1. Take the top few thousand by score.
  2. Cluster them (Volume 07, Lesson 04).
  3. Take the best 2–3 from each cluster — so you buy many hypotheses, not one.
  4. Apply ADMET and structural filters now, not earlier. A compound that fails a filter but scores superbly is worth a human look.
  5. A chemist looks at every structure. This step is never skipped, and it is not a formality.
Why the human step survives

Because a chemist spots in two seconds things no filter encodes: a compound that will decompose in DMSO, a reactive group in a context your SMARTS missed, a structure that is obviously a dye, a compound that looks like something the group tried in 2019. Expect roughly 10–30% of your computational picks to be rejected on sight, and treat that as information about your filters rather than as an insult.

Part II

ADMET

Lesson 07

Why ADMET matters more than potency

The historical lesson

In the early 1990s, a large share of clinical failures were pharmacokinetic — the compound worked in a test tube and never reached its target in a human. The industry responded by screening ADMET properties early, and PK-driven attrition fell substantially.

What replaced it

Attrition did not go away; it moved. Today the dominant causes are lack of efficacy and safety. But the lesson stands and generalises:

Potency is the easiest property to optimise and the least likely to be what kills you. Chemists can usually make something more potent. Making it soluble, absorbable, stable in the body, and non-toxic — while keeping the potency — is the hard part.

What this means for your models

An ADMET model that flags a problem in month three is worth more than a potency model that gains 0.3 log units. It is also usually easier to build well: ADMET endpoints have large, consistent public datasets measured by standard assays, which is exactly what Volume 08 said chemical data usually lacks.

Lesson 09

hERG — the liability worth understanding properly

Worked example · why one channel gets its own lesson 
WHAT IT IS
  hERG is a potassium channel in heart muscle. It helps reset the
  electrical cycle of each heartbeat.

WHAT GOES WRONG
  Block it  ->  the reset is delayed  ->  the QT interval on an ECG
  lengthens  ->  risk of torsades de pointes, a potentially fatal
  arrhythmia.

WHY IT IS SO COMMON
  The hERG channel's binding site is large, greasy and accommodating.
  It binds an unusually wide range of molecules -- and the features
  that make a compound bind hERG (basic nitrogen, aromatic rings,
  lipophilicity) are the SAME features chemists add to improve
  potency and permeability.

  So hERG liability tends to INCREASE as a project optimises
  everything else. It is not an unlucky accident; it is a
  structural tension in the optimisation.

THE HISTORY
  Several drugs -- terfenadine and cisapride among them -- were
  withdrawn or restricted over QT prolongation. Regulators now
  require cardiac safety assessment for essentially every new drug.

⭐ CONSEQUENCE FOR YOU: hERG is checked EARLY and often, and a
   hERG model is one of the most-used models in any pharma
   company. It is also a good first ADMET model to build: the
   public data is plentiful and the endpoint is well defined.
Lesson 10

Modelling ADMET — what is different

Compared with a QSAR model…What changes
The data is betterStandard assays, run consistently, often thousands of compounds. Less between-lab chaos than literature IC₅₀ data.
The data is more diverseADMET is measured on compounds from many projects, so the applicability domain is genuinely broad — a rare luxury.
Physicochemical descriptors work wellSolubility and permeability are driven by bulk properties — size, polarity, greasiness — so descriptors carry real signal and interpretability, unlike in target binding.
Classification often beats regressionThe decision is usually "is this a problem?" — a three-bin answer (low / medium / high risk) is more honest and more useful than a precise number nobody believes.
Being wrong costs differentlyA false negative on hERG lets a dangerous compound through. A false positive kills a good one. These are not symmetric, and the threshold should reflect that — usually by being conservative on safety endpoints.
Off-the-shelf options

Several free tools — SwissADME, pkCSM, ADMETlab among them — predict a panel of these endpoints from a SMILES. Use them for orientation and never as evidence. Their applicability domains are undocumented for your chemistry, and their confident single numbers invite exactly the overinterpretation Volume 08 warned about. If a property matters to the project, build the model on data you can inspect.

Lesson 11

Multi-parameter optimisation

A real compound must be potent and soluble and permeable and stable and not hit hERG. Five objectives that trade against each other — adding lipophilicity improves permeability and worsens solubility and hERG.

The naive approach, and why it fails

Combine everything into one weighted score and rank by it. The problem is that the weights are invented, and a single number hides which property is failing — so the chemist cannot act on it. A compound scoring 0.4 because it is insoluble and one scoring 0.4 because it hits hERG need completely different responses.

What works better: the property profile 
compound   pIC50   logS    perm   CYP3A4   hERG    verdict
   A        8.2    -5.8    high    clean    5.1 uM   promising
   B        8.9    -7.1    low     inhib    0.8 uM   potent, otherwise bad
   C        7.1    -4.2    high    clean   >30 uM    weaker, clean --> OPTIMISE THIS

⭐ COMPOUND C IS THE RIGHT STARTING POINT, and a single combined
   score would have ranked B first. Potency is the easiest property
   to improve; a clean safety profile is the hardest to recover.
The two techniques worth knowing

Pareto fronts. Find the compounds that are not beaten on every objective by some other compound. That set is the honest answer to "what are my options?", and it requires no invented weights.

Desirability functions. If you must have one number, map each property onto 0–1 through a curve that encodes what "good" means for that property, then combine. Better than raw weighting because the curves are explicit and arguable — but always keep the components beside the score.

Try it · Your screening funnel takes 2 million compounds down to 50. The team is pleased. What single number should you report alongside it?
Answer

The enrichment — how many more actives are in your 50 than chance would give — and, if you cannot estimate it, say so plainly.

WHY THE FUNNEL ALONE PROVES NOTHING
   Any set of filters reduces 2,000,000 to 50. Sorting by
   molecular weight and taking the top 50 does it too. The
   reduction is not the achievement — the ENRICHMENT is.

WHERE THE NUMBER COMES FROM
   Retrospective validation: known actives spiked into
   property-matched decoys, run through the SAME funnel.
   Report enrichment at 1%, because the top of the list is
   the only part anyone will buy.

AND THE HONEST VERSION WHEN YOU CANNOT
   "We have no known actives for this target, so the funnel
    is unvalidated. These 50 are a prioritised hypothesis,
    not an enriched set."
   ⭐ That sentence costs nothing and protects everyone —
      including you, in six months.

The second number worth volunteering: how many distinct scaffolds are in the 50? If the answer is four, you are proposing to test four ideas, not fifty.

Lesson 12

Colloidal aggregation — the artefact behind many false positives

The mechanism, because it explains the whole problem

Some compounds clump together in aqueous buffer into colloidal aggregates — particles tens to hundreds of nanometres across. Those particles adsorb protein onto their surface and sequester it. The enzyme stops working, the assay reports inhibition, and the compound looks like a hit while having no specific interaction with the target at all.

  WHY IT MATTERS MORE THAN ITS REPUTATION SUGGESTS

    · it is one of the largest single sources of false
      positives in high-throughput screening
    · it is CONCENTRATION-DEPENDENT, so it appears at screening
      concentrations and vanishes on follow-up — which reads as
      "irreproducible assay" rather than "aggregation"
    · aggregators are often flat, greasy and poorly soluble —
      exactly the molecules that also score well in docking

  THE RISK PROFILE TO FLAG

    high logP  +  low aqueous solubility  +  flat aromatic
    system  +  activity that does not track with structure
    across close analogues

  ⭐ THE ASSAY-SIDE TEST, WHICH YOU SHOULD KNOW EVEN THOUGH
     YOU DO NOT RUN IT
     Re-run the assay with a small amount of detergent
     (typically 0.01% Triton X-100). Detergent breaks up the
     colloid. If the "activity" disappears, it was aggregation.
     Asking a biologist "was this run with detergent?" is one
     of the highest-value questions a computational person can
     ask in a hit-triage meeting.
What you can do computationally

Estimate an aggregation risk from the property profile and carry it as a flag beside each hit, exactly like PAINS. Do not filter on it — aggregation is concentration-dependent and a prediction is a suspicion, not a verdict. But a hit list that arrives already annotated with "these four are aggregation risks, suggest testing with detergent" is the kind of deliverable that gets a computational person invited back.
