QSAR. The first real model, and the four things that make it honest.

Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship: predict activity from structure. The modelling itself is ten lines of scikit-learn. Everything that distinguishes a useful model from a self-deceiving one is in the split, the metric, the baseline and the applicability domain — so that is where this volume spends its length.

Part I

The model

Lesson 01

What QSAR is

  MOLECULE        -->  REPRESENTATION  -->  MODEL  -->  PREDICTED ACTIVITY
  (Volume 03)          (Volume 05)          here       (pIC50, or active/inactive)

  THE ASSUMPTION UNDERNEATH IT (Volume 05, Lesson 12):
      similar structure -> similar activity

  SO QSAR IS, IN EFFECT, A SOPHISTICATED NEAREST-NEIGHBOUR ARGUMENT.
  Everything that limits it follows from that: it works where you have
  neighbours, and fails where you do not — which is precisely what the
  applicability domain formalises.
Worked example · the whole model, in twelve lines 
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import r2_score, mean_absolute_error
from rdkit.Chem import rdFingerprintGenerator

gen = rdFingerprintGenerator.GetMorganGenerator(radius=2, fpSize=2048)
X = np.array([gen.GetFingerprintAsNumPy(m) for m in df["mol"]])
y = df["pIC50"].values

train_idx, test_idx = scaffold_split(df, frac_train=0.8)     # NOT train_test_split

model = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=500, n_jobs=-1, random_state=42)
model.fit(X[train_idx], y[train_idx])
pred = model.predict(X[test_idx])

print("R2 ", round(r2_score(y[test_idx], pred), 3))
print("MAE", round(mean_absolute_error(y[test_idx], pred), 3))

That is the entire modelling step, and it is the easy part. The one line that matters is scaffold_split rather than train_test_split, and the rest of this volume is about why.

Lesson 02

The baseline you must define first

Define it before training anything

An R² of 0.55 is meaningless in isolation. It is only interpretable against something, and the something must be chosen before you see your model's score — otherwise you will pick whichever baseline makes your number look good.

BaselineWhat beating it provesDifficulty
Predict the meanAlmost nothing — R² = 0 by construction.Trivial. But compute it, so the MAE has a reference.
Nearest neighbour by TanimotoThat your model adds something beyond "find the most similar known compound".The honest baseline, and surprisingly hard to beat. Use this one.
Physicochemical descriptors + linear modelThat the structural detail in fingerprints is earning its place.Cheap, interpretable, sometimes wins outright on physical properties.
Random forest on MorganNothing — this is the standard. It is the baseline that fancier methods must beat.The bar for any deep learning claim in Volume 10.
The nearest-neighbour baseline, concretely 
For each test compound:
    find the most similar TRAINING compound by Tanimoto
    predict that compound's activity

If your model does not beat this, it has not learned a
structure-activity RELATIONSHIP. It has learned a lookup table.
Lesson 03

Which algorithm — and why the answer is boring

AlgorithmGood whenNotes
Random forestAlmost always, on 100–10,000 compoundsThe default. Handles sparse binary features, needs almost no tuning, gives a usable uncertainty estimate from tree variance. Hard to beat.
Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM)You have a few thousand compounds and time to tuneUsually a small improvement over RF, at the cost of tuning and more overfitting risk on small data.
SVM (RBF or Tanimoto kernel)Small datasets, a few hundred compoundsHistorically strong on QSAR. Needs feature scaling and careful tuning.
Ridge / PLSDescriptors, and you need interpretabilityPLS is the traditional QSAR workhorse and still reasonable for physical properties.
Graph neural networks> 50,000 compounds, or multi-taskVolume 10. Below that scale they typically do not beat a random forest, and the literature overstates how often they do.
The finding that keeps being rediscovered

On typical medicinal chemistry datasets — hundreds to low thousands of compounds — random forests on Morgan fingerprints remain competitive with much more sophisticated methods. Papers claiming large deep-learning wins often compare against a poorly tuned baseline, or use a random split. When both are done properly the gap usually narrows sharply or disappears. Knowing this saves you months.

Lesson 04

Why random splits lie

Worked example · the same model, two splits 
SAME DATA. SAME MODEL. SAME FEATURES. DIFFERENT SPLIT.

  random 80/20 split          R2 = 0.82     "excellent model"
  scaffold 80/20 split        R2 = 0.41     "modest model"
  time-based split            R2 = 0.28     "barely useful"

WHICH NUMBER IS TRUE?

  All three. They answer DIFFERENT QUESTIONS:

  random     "how well does it predict compounds from series it has
              already seen most of?"                    -- rarely useful
  scaffold   "how well does it predict a NEW series?"    -- usually the real question
  time       "how well would it have predicted what the
              project did next?"                        -- the honest simulation

Why the gap is so large: chemical datasets are built from series. A chemist makes a core structure and twenty variations around it. Split at random and roughly sixteen variations land in training and four in test — and predicting those four requires recognising the series, not understanding the chemistry.

The model has memorised a scaffold and been rewarded for it. This is not a subtle statistical concern; it is the single most common reason a model that looked good in development is useless on the project's next compounds.

Lesson 05

The four splits, and when each is right

SplitHow it worksUse when
RandomShuffle, cut.Almost never for chemistry. Legitimate only if the deployment population really is drawn from the same series — rare. Report it as an upper bound if you report it at all.
ScaffoldGroup by Murcko scaffold; whole groups go to one side.The default. Simulates "a new chemical series arrives".
ClusterButina-cluster, then split by cluster.Stricter than scaffold, since it also separates different scaffolds that are structurally close. Good when scaffolds are too fine-grained.
TimeTrain on everything before a date, test on everything after.The most honest, when you have dates. It is exactly what deployment looks like, and it captures series drift that scaffold splits miss.
The corollary most people miss

Cross-validation must respect the grouping too. A scaffold split followed by ordinary k-fold CV inside the training set leaks scaffolds between folds, and your hyperparameter choice is then tuned on a leak. Use GroupKFold with the scaffold as the group — this is a one-word change that people routinely omit.

Lesson 06

Writing a scaffold split

Worked example · the function, and the invariant it must satisfy 
from collections import defaultdict
from rdkit.Chem.Scaffolds import MurckoScaffold

def scaffold_split(mols, frac_train=0.8):
    # 1. group molecule indices by scaffold
    groups = defaultdict(list)
    for i, m in enumerate(mols):
        scaf = Chem.MolToSmiles(MurckoScaffold.GetScaffoldForMol(m))
        groups[scaf].append(i)

    # 2. biggest groups first — so the largest series lands in TRAIN
    ordered = sorted(groups.values(), key=len, reverse=True)

    # 3. fill train until it is full, then everything else is test
    n_train = int(frac_train * len(mols))
    train, test = [], []
    for g in ordered:
        (train if len(train) + len(g) <= n_train else test).extend(g)
    return train, test


# THE INVARIANT — assert it, every time
def scaffolds_of(idx):
    return {Chem.MolToSmiles(MurckoScaffold.GetScaffoldForMol(mols[i])) for i in idx}

assert not (scaffolds_of(train) & scaffolds_of(test))

Why biggest-first: putting the largest series in training and the rarer scaffolds in test makes the test set deliberately harder and more like reality — you are usually asked about unusual compounds, not about more of what you already have.

Why the assert matters: a scaffold split that silently fails still produces a number, and the number looks fine. This is the bug profile that does real damage, so the invariant is checked rather than assumed. It was the fourth test in Volume 07 for the same reason.

Try it · Your scaffold split gives R² = 0.41. Your manager says "the random split gave 0.82, use that one, it's the same data". What do you say?
Answer

Do not argue about statistics. Reframe it as a question about what the model is for.

"Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions. The 0.82 tells us how well we predict more compounds from series we already know. The 0.41 tells us how well we predict a new series. Which one the project needs depends on what we are going to do with it — and since we are planning to use it on new chemistry, 0.41 is the number that predicts our experience."

Then make it concrete and testable: "if we report 0.82 and it performs at 0.41 in practice, we lose the chemists' trust and they stop using it." That is the real cost, and it is an argument about outcomes rather than about methodology.

Offer the constructive path too: report both, clearly labelled, and hold out the project's genuinely most recent compounds as a third, honest check. Being the person who reports both numbers with their meanings is how you build the credibility that makes your next recommendation land.

The metric

Lesson 07

Regression metrics

MetricSaysUse when
RMSETypical error, in pIC₅₀ units, punishing large misses.You care about magnitude. Report this — it is interpretable. RMSE 0.7 means "typically wrong by a factor of five".
MAEMedian-ish error, robust to outliers.Alongside RMSE. If MAE ≪ RMSE, a few compounds are badly wrong — go and look at them.
R²Fraction of variance explained.Conventional, but depends on the spread of your test set: a narrow-range test set gives a low R² even with good predictions. Never report it alone.
Spearman ρRank correlation.Often the metric that matters most, because the decision is usually "which do I make first?" — a ranking question, not a magnitude one.
Lesson 08

Classification metrics — and why accuracy is useless here

Worked example · the base-rate trap, in chemistry 
A screening library: 100,000 compounds, 1,000 of them active (1%).

MODEL A: predicts "inactive" for everything.
    accuracy = 99.0%              <-- and it is completely worthless

MODEL B: picks 500 compounds, 100 of them active.
    accuracy = 99.1%              <-- barely different!
    but: hit rate 20% vs 1% at random  =  20x ENRICHMENT
    this model just saved the project a great deal of money

⭐ ACCURACY CANNOT TELL THESE APART. Never use it on
   imbalanced chemical data, which is all chemical data.
MetricWhat it is for
ROC-AUCOverall ranking quality. Conventional and comparable — but insensitive to the top of the list, which is the only part you will act on.
PR-AUCBetter than ROC-AUC under heavy imbalance. Its baseline is the active rate, so a PR-AUC of 0.15 at a 1% active rate is a strong result.
Enrichment factor at 1%The field's own metric. "How many times more actives in my top 1% than at random?" Directly answers the business question.
BEDROCLike ROC-AUC but weighted towards the top of the ranking. Has a tunable α; report it, because the value changes the number a lot.
MCCA balanced single number for a fixed threshold. Better than F1 when both classes matter.
The rule for choosing

Pick the metric that matches the decision. If you will buy the top 500, measure the top 500 — enrichment at that cut, or precision at k. A model with a worse ROC-AUC but better top-100 precision is the better model for that decision, and reporting only ROC-AUC would have hidden it.

Lesson 09

The noise ceiling

You cannot be more accurate than your labels

From Volume 06: the median spread among replicate measurements was 0.31 log units. Between different labs, differences of 0.5 log units or more are common for IC₅₀.

So an RMSE below roughly 0.5 on literature-derived IC₅₀ data should be treated as a leak until proven otherwise, not as a triumph. The usual causes: duplicates spanning the split, a random split, or the same compound in train and test under two tautomers.

Report the noise floor next to the model's RMSE. "RMSE 0.62 against an experimental noise floor of 0.31" is a far more informative and more credible sentence than "RMSE 0.62", and it tells the reader how much room is left.

Honesty

Lesson 10

Applicability domain — the field's defining idea

The problem it solves

A model will return a number for any molecule you give it, including one utterly unlike anything it was trained on. It has no way of refusing. Feed a random peptide to a kinase-inhibitor model and you get a confident pIC₅₀ that means nothing.

The definition

The applicability domain is the region of chemical space where the model's predictions are supported by training data. Reporting a prediction from outside it, without saying so, is the field's cardinal sin — and it is the fastest way to lose a chemist's trust permanently.

How to compute one — the simple version that works 
def in_domain(test_fp, train_fps, threshold=0.35):
    sims = DataStructs.BulkTanimotoSimilarity(test_fp, train_fps)
    return max(sims) >= threshold          # nearest training neighbour

# calibrate the threshold on data you have, do not guess it:
#   bin the TEST compounds by their max-similarity to train,
#   plot prediction error against that bin,
#   the similarity at which error rises sharply IS your threshold.

Other approaches — distance to the training centroid, leverage, the variance across random forest trees, or a conformal predictor for calibrated intervals — are more sophisticated. The nearest-neighbour version above captures most of the value and is easy to explain.

What you actually ship

Predictions with a flag, and a refusal for the worst cases.

compound   predicted pIC50   nearest train sim   status
  A            7.8                0.62           IN DOMAIN
  B            6.4                0.41           IN DOMAIN
  C            8.9                0.19           ** OUT OF DOMAIN — do not use **

Compound C is the dangerous one: the highest predicted activity, and the least support. That pattern is common, because extrapolation tends to produce extreme values — which is exactly why the most exciting prediction deserves the most scrutiny.

Lesson 11

Y-randomisation — the ten-minute sanity check

Worked example · proving the signal is real 
real_score = evaluate(model, X, y)                 # e.g. R2 = 0.41

scores = []
for _ in range(50):
    y_shuffled = np.random.permutation(y)          # break the structure-activity link
    scores.append(evaluate(fresh_model(), X, y_shuffled))

print("real:", real_score)
print("shuffled:", np.mean(scores), "+/-", np.std(scores))

  real:      0.41
  shuffled: -0.02 +/- 0.05        <-- GOOD. the signal is real.

  real:      0.41
  shuffled:  0.33 +/- 0.08        <-- ALARM. your pipeline can "predict"
                                      random labels. Something leaks.

The second case means you have a leak, and it is nearly always one of three things: duplicates spanning the split, feature selection performed before splitting, or a target that is partly encoded in the features. Y-randomisation cannot tell you which — but it tells you to stop and look, which is the point.

Run it once per project. It costs ten minutes and it has saved a great many people from presenting a model built on a leak.

Lesson 12

The OECD principles — the checklist regulators use

#PrincipleIn practice
1A defined endpoint"IC₅₀, human AChE, binding assay" — not "activity". Volume 06, Lesson 03.
2An unambiguous algorithmAnyone can reproduce it: features, parameters, versions, seed. Volume 02, Lesson 10.
3A defined domain of applicabilityLesson 10. Note that this is a requirement, not a nicety.
4Appropriate measures of goodness-of-fit, robustness and predictivityThree distinct things: fit on training, stability under resampling and Y-randomisation, and performance on a proper external test set.
5A mechanistic interpretation, if possible"If possible" is doing real work — but being able to say why the model predicts what it does is what makes a chemist act on it.
Why this matters even outside regulation

These five principles were written for regulatory acceptance of QSAR models, but they are simply a good checklist for any model. Auditing yourself against them before presenting is the cheapest way to find the hole someone else would have found.

Lesson 13

Interpretation — making a chemist act on it

Feature importance

Which fingerprint bits mattered. Then map the bits back to substructures and draw them. A picture of "the model likes this fragment" is worth more than any table. Beware bit collisions from Volume 05 — a collided bit gives a confident, wrong explanation.

SHAP values

Per-prediction attribution: why this compound got this score. More useful than global importance, because the chemist's question is always about a specific molecule.

Similar training compounds

The most underrated. Alongside every prediction, show the three most similar training compounds and their measured values. A chemist can evaluate that instantly, and it makes the applicability domain visible rather than abstract.

Matched molecular pairs

From Volume 07. Often more actionable than the model itself, and it requires no trust in a black box.
