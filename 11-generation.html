AI cheminformatics · Volume 11 of 17 · phase 2

Generation and retrosynthesis. Designing molecules instead of ranking them.

Every volume so far has taken molecules that already exist and sorted them. This one invents new ones. That sounds like the exciting part, and it is — but it comes with a bill that nobody warns beginners about: a generated molecule is a picture until someone can make it. So half this volume is generation, and half is the harder question of whether the thing you generated can exist outside a screen.

Part I

Making molecules up

Lesson 01

From ranking to designing

Volume 09 built a screening funnel: take a catalogue of ten million molecules that already exist, score them, keep the best thousand. That approach has a hard ceiling — you can only ever find what is already in the catalogue.

  VIRTUAL SCREENING  (Volume 09)

     [ 10 million existing molecules ]
                 |
            score each
                 |
         [ keep the best 1,000 ]

     ⭐ CEILING: the best molecule you can find is the best
        molecule someone already made.


  GENERATIVE DESIGN  (this volume)

     [ a model that has read 1.5 million drug-like molecules ]
                 |
          invent new ones
                 |
     [ 1,000 molecules that have never existed ]
                 |
          ⚠ and may never exist

The size of the prize is easy to state. The number of drug-like molecules that could exist is usually quoted at around 1060. Every molecule ever made by every chemist in history is somewhere around 108. Screening searches the 108. Generation is an attempt at the rest.

The catch, stated once, early

A generative model optimises whatever you score. It does not know what a chemist is. If your scoring function says "high predicted activity, low molecular weight", it will happily hand you a molecule with six adjacent quaternary carbons that no reaction on earth can assemble — and it will score beautifully. Everything in Part IV exists because of this sentence.

Lesson 02

SMILES as a language

The simplest generative approach reuses a trick from text. A SMILES string is a sequence of characters obeying rules — like a sentence. So train a model to predict the next character, the way a phone keyboard predicts the next word, then let it write its own strings.

Worked example · how a model writes CCO 
The model has read ~1.5M SMILES from ChEMBL. It has learned
nothing about chemistry — only which characters follow which.

STEP 1   context: "<START>"
         model's guesses:  C 62%   O 9%   N 8%   c 7%   ...
         sample -> C

STEP 2   context: "<START>C"
         model's guesses:  C 41%   (  14%   O 11%   =  6%  ...
         sample -> C

STEP 3   context: "<START>CC"
         model's guesses:  O 22%   C 19%   N 14%  <END> 9% ...
         sample -> O

STEP 4   context: "<START>CCO"
         model's guesses:  <END> 71%   C 12%  ...
         sample -> <END>

RESULT   "CCO"  -> Chem.MolFromSmiles("CCO") -> ethanol. Valid.

⭐ NOTE WHAT DID NOT HAPPEN: nobody told the model that carbon
   makes four bonds. It learned that "CC(C)(C)(C)C" never appears
   in the training data, so it assigns that pattern a low
   probability. Valency is learned as a STATISTICAL HABIT,
   not enforced as a RULE.

That last point is the whole character of this approach, and it explains its main failure. Because valency is only a habit, the model breaks it sometimes: an unclosed ring, a five-bonded carbon, a bracket that never closes. Those strings fail to parse and are simply discarded. A well-trained model produces valid strings above 95% of the time; early ones managed 70%.

Why this works better than it has any right to

It is genuinely surprising that a model with no chemistry in it produces chemistry. The explanation is that SMILES is a very constrained language — far more constrained than English. There are roughly 40 usable characters and the grammar is rigid, so with a million examples the statistical habits end up encoding most of the real rules.

Lesson 03

Temperature — the one sampling knob to understand

At each step the model gives a probability for every possible next character. How you pick from that distribution decides what kind of output you get, and beginners routinely leave it at the default and then wonder why every generated molecule looks the same.

Worked example · the same step, three temperatures 
Model's raw guesses after "<START>C":
        C 41%    ( 14%    O 11%    N 9%    = 6%    others 19%

TEMPERATURE 0.5 — sharpen the distribution (confident)
        C 68%    ( 12%    O  8%    N 5%    = 2%    others 5%
        -> almost always picks C
        -> output: safe, drug-like, REPETITIVE

TEMPERATURE 1.0 — use it exactly as the model gave it
        C 41%    ( 14%    O 11%    N 9%    = 6%    others 19%
        -> the honest middle. Sensible default.

TEMPERATURE 1.5 — flatten the distribution (adventurous)
        C 27%    ( 15%    O 13%    N 12%   = 10%   others 23%
        -> picks unusual characters more often
        -> output: novel, weirder, MORE INVALID STRINGS

⭐ THE TRADE, IN ONE LINE
   low temperature  = valid and boring
   high temperature = interesting and often broken

   If your generated set has 99.9% validity and 30% uniqueness,
   your temperature is too low. That is not a good model —
   it is a model reciting its training data.
Lesson 04

Generating the graph instead of the string

If broken valency is the problem with strings, an obvious fix is to build the molecule as a graph — adding atoms and bonds directly, checking valency at every step. Then invalid molecules are impossible rather than merely unlikely.

ApproachHow it buildsThe trade
SMILES RNN / TransformerOne character at a timeSimple, fast, well understood. ~95%+ validity. The workhorse, and what REINVENT uses.
Graph autoregressiveOne atom or bond at a time, valency checkedValidity by construction. Slower, more complex, and the ordering of atoms turns out to matter more than expected.
JT-VAE (junction tree)Assembles from a vocabulary of valid fragments — rings and functional groups100% validity, and fragments are chemically sensible. But it can only build from fragments it has seen.
The trap in that 100%

JT-VAE advertises 100% validity, and beginners read that as "the best model". It is not a quality measure — it is a statement about the construction method. A model that assembles molecules from a fixed fragment vocabulary cannot produce an invalid molecule, in the same way that a Lego set cannot produce a broken brick. When validity is guaranteed by construction, the metric carries no information and you must judge the model on uniqueness, novelty and synthesizability instead.

Part II

Steering it

Lesson 05

REINVENT, and the leash

A model trained on ChEMBL generates drug-like molecules. It does not generate molecules that hit your target. Getting from one to the other is what REINVENT (Olivecrona et al., 2017; Blaschke et al., 2020) does, and it is the tool actually used in pharma — AstraZeneca, Novartis, Merck. If you learn one generative system, learn this one.

  THE FOUR PARTS

    1. PRIOR      a SMILES model pretrained on ChEMBL.
                  Knows what "drug-like" looks like. FROZEN — never changes.

    2. AGENT      starts as an exact copy of the Prior.
                  This is the one that learns.

    3. SCORING    any function: a QSAR model, a docking score,
       FUNCTION   a rule, or a mix. Returns a number in [0, 1].

    4. RL LOOP    agent generates  ->  score them  ->  nudge the
                  agent toward the high scorers  ->  repeat.

  THE LOSS, IN WORDS

    loss = -(score x how likely the agent was to write it)
           + sigma x (how far the agent has drifted from the prior)
                     ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
                     ⭐ THE LEASH
What the leash is for

Without the second term the agent will find a degenerate way to score well and collapse onto it — generating the same molecule, or an absurd one that happens to fool the scoring model. The prior term says: "stay recognisably drug-like while you optimise."

The parameter

sigma sets how hard the leash pulls. The standard value is 128. Too low and the agent wanders off into nonsense that scores well; too high and it never moves away from the prior and you get no optimisation at all.

What juniors do

Leave sigma at the default, watch the score climb, and report success. The score climbing is not the result — the score is the thing you asked it to maximise, so of course it climbed. The result is whether the molecules are any good, which the score cannot tell you, because the score is exactly what was gamed.

The question to be able to answer

"Your reward went from 0.2 to 0.9 — how do you know the model did not just learn to exploit your scoring function?" The answer involves holding out a scorer, checking the molecules against a second model, and looking at them with a chemist.

Lesson 06

The scoring function is the whole experiment

Beginners treat the scoring function as configuration. It is not — it is the hypothesis. The model will satisfy exactly what you wrote down, including the parts you did not mean.

Worked example · a scoring function, and how it gets gamed 
WHAT YOU WROTE
    score = 0.6 x QSAR_activity(mol)      # want it active
          + 0.2 x (1 if MW < 500 else 0)  # want it small
          + 0.2 x (1 if logP < 5 else 0)  # want it not greasy

WHAT THE MODEL FOUND
    A molecule scoring 0.94 that:
      - sits far outside the QSAR model's applicability domain,
        where the model extrapolates and predicts high activity
        with no evidence at all              (Volume 08, Lesson 11)
      - has MW 499                            (the cliff edge)
      - has logP 4.98                         (the cliff edge)

⭐ TWO LESSONS

  1. HARD CUTOFFS GET SAT ON. A step function at 500 tells the
     model that 499 is perfect and 501 is worthless. Use a smooth
     ramp instead, so 520 is slightly worse rather than fatal.

  2. AN UNGUARDED PREDICTOR IS AN INVITATION. Any QSAR model in a
     scoring function MUST carry an applicability-domain check, or
     the generator will find the region where the model is
     confidently wrong and live there. This is the single most
     common way a generative campaign fails.
Lesson 07

Wanting six things at once

Real projects never optimise one property. They want potency and solubility and metabolic stability and no hERG liability and synthesizability — and several of those genuinely fight each other.

MethodWhat it doesWhen it is the right choice
Weighted sumMultiply each property by a weight, add them upSimple and usually where you start. Its flaw: a high score can hide a fatal weakness, because a brilliant potency score can pay for an unacceptable toxicity score.
Weighted productMultiply the scores togetherBetter when any single failure should be fatal — one zero makes the whole thing zero. Closer to how a project actually decides.
ParetoKeeps every molecule that is not beaten on all properties at onceWhen you genuinely do not know the trade-off yet, and want to hand chemists a set of options instead of one answer.
The intuition for Pareto, without the maths

Molecule A is potent but poorly soluble. Molecule B is soluble but weaker. Neither is beaten on everything, so both survive — they sit on the Pareto front. But molecule C, which is both weaker than A and less soluble than A, is beaten on every axis and is discarded. The front is the set of honest options; picking among them is a project decision, not a computational one.

Lesson 08

Keeping the scaffold you already like

Most real design work is not "invent a molecule from nothing". It is "we have a compound that works; give me variations that keep the core and fix the solubility". That is scaffold decoration, and it is the mode chemists actually ask for.

Worked example · what you hand the model 
THE SCAFFOLD (fixed — the part that must not change)

    c1ccc2[nH]ccc2c1        an indole core, say

THE REQUEST
    "Fill these two attachment points. Keep MW under 450.
     Improve predicted solubility. Do not touch the core."

WHAT THE MODEL GENERATES
    c1ccc2[nH]c(CCN)cc2c1        + an aminoethyl arm
    c1ccc2[nH]c(CCO)cc2c1        + a hydroxyethyl arm
    c1ccc2[nH]c(CC(=O)N)cc2c1    + an amide arm
                ^^^^^^^^^ only this part varies

⭐ WHY THIS IS THE MOST USEFUL MODE IN PRACTICE

  · the core is already known to bind — you keep what works
  · the search space is small enough to explore properly
  · the results are immediately recognisable to a chemist,
    which matters more than it sounds: a suggestion a medicinal
    chemist can read in two seconds gets acted on. A novel
    scaffold from nowhere gets ignored, however well it scores.
Part III

Is any of it good?

Try it · Your generator reports 99.9% validity, 100% novelty and a reward that rose from 0.2 to 0.9. Your manager calls it a success. Is it?
Answer

None of those three numbers is evidence of anything useful, and two of them are mildly worrying.

VALIDITY 99.9%   ⚠ suspiciously high for a SMILES model.
                 Either the temperature is very low — so the
                 model is reciting training data — or validity
                 is guaranteed by construction, in which case
                 the number carries no information.

NOVELTY 100%     measured against the TRAINING SET only. Every
                 molecule could still be in ChEMBL, or patented.

REWARD 0.2 -> 0.9  the model maximised the function I wrote.
                 That is what optimisation does. It says
                 nothing about whether the molecules are good,
                 because the score is precisely what was gamed.

THE NUMBER NOBODY QUOTED: UNIQUENESS.
   With 99.9% validity and a rising reward, my first check is
   uniqueness and distinct scaffold count. If uniqueness is
   30% and 10,000 molecules gave 12 scaffolds, this is mode
   collapse being reported as success.

The answer that closes it: "how many of them have a solved retrosynthetic route?" Until that number exists, there is no result — only a picture gallery.

Lesson 09

The four metrics, and what each misses

Validity
The fraction of generated strings that parse into a molecule. Target > 95%. Uninformative when the method guarantees it by construction.
Uniqueness
Of the valid ones, the fraction that are distinct after canonicalisation. Target > 90%. Low uniqueness is the fingerprint of mode collapse.
Novelty
The fraction not present in the training set. Target > 80%. Measures novelty against your training data only — a molecule can be "novel" here and well known in the literature.
Synthesizability
Whether it can actually be made. Not part of the standard trio, and the reason Part IV exists.
The sentence to internalise

These metrics are necessary but nowhere near sufficient. A model can score 100% validity, 100% uniqueness and 100% novelty by generating a million distinct, parseable, unprecedented molecules that are all unmakeable garbage. Every one of those numbers would be perfect. The report would look excellent. The output would be worth nothing.

Lesson 10

Mode collapse — how to spot it yourself

Mode collapse is when the model finds one region that scores well and stops exploring. It is the most common failure of a reinforcement-learning run, and it is easy to miss because the reward curve looks better as it happens.

Worked example · reading the symptoms 
RUN REPORT, EPOCH 400

    mean reward ................ 0.91   ↑ (was 0.22)
    validity ................... 99.8%
    uniqueness ................. 31%      ⚠
    mean pairwise Tanimoto ..... 0.78     ⚠⚠
    distinct Murcko scaffolds .. 12       ⚠⚠⚠
      (out of 10,000 generated molecules)

READ IT LIKE THIS

  reward up + uniqueness down     = collapsing
  pairwise similarity above ~0.6  = they are all the same
                                    molecule wearing hats
  12 scaffolds from 10,000        = conclusive

⭐ THE HABIT: never judge a run by its reward curve. Track
   uniqueness and scaffold count ON THE SAME CHART as reward.
   Collapse is obvious the moment you plot them together, and
   invisible if you do not.

THE USUAL FIXES
  · raise sigma — pull the agent back toward the prior
  · add a diversity term to the score itself
  · penalise molecules similar to ones already generated
    ("experience replay" with a similarity penalty)
  · stop earlier — the best set is often 200 epochs before
    the best reward
Lesson 11

The benchmarks, and why not to trust a leaderboard

SuiteWhat it measuresThe limitation
GuacaMol
Brown et al., 2019		Distribution learning plus goal-directed tasks — rediscover a known drug, optimise a stated propertyThe goal-directed tasks are scored by the same computed property being optimised, so a model that games the property wins.
MOSES
Polykovskiy et al., 2020		Distribution learning — does the generated set look statistically like real drug-like chemistrySays nothing about whether the molecules are useful, only whether they resemble the training distribution.
Use them for what they are

These suites are good at one job: telling you your implementation is not broken. If your model scores far below published numbers on MOSES, you have a bug. If it scores at or above them, you have a working implementation — and still no evidence that it will produce a useful molecule for your project. Benchmarks validate machinery, not discoveries.

Part IV

Can it actually be made?

Lesson 12

The paper-molecule problem

This is the part of the field where computational people most often embarrass themselves, so it is worth being blunt about the mechanism.

  WHAT HAPPENS

    generative model  ->  1,000 molecules, all scoring > 0.9
                              |
                     hand them to a chemist
                              |
    ┌─────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┐
    │  "I cannot make any of these."                     │
    └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  WHY IT HAPPENS

    The model optimises a SCORE. Nothing in that score knows
    about reactions. The model has no concept of a synthesis,
    a starting material, a protecting group, or a yield.
    It draws. It does not build.

  ⭐ THE COST, CONCRETELY
     A medicinal chemist's time reviewing garbage is expensive,
     but the real cost is credibility: a computational group that
     hands over unmakeable molecules twice does not get asked a
     third time.
Lesson 13

SA Score and SCScore

SA ScoreSCScore
SourceErtl & Schuffenhauer, 2009 · in RDKit Contrib as sascorerColey et al., 2018 · a small neural network
Range1 (easy) → 10 (hard)1 (simple) → 5 (complex)
HowHeuristic: how common are this molecule's fragments in PubChem, plus penalties for rings, stereocentres and sizeLearned from reaction data — trained so that a product always scores higher than its reactants
Blind spotOptimistic about molecules built from common fragments joined in an uncommon way — every piece looks familiar, the assembly is impossibleOptimistic about anything resembling its training reactions; inherits the historical bias of what chemists have already made
Worked example · reading SA Score honestly 
from rdkit import Chem
from rdkit.Chem import RDConfig
import sys, os
sys.path.append(os.path.join(RDConfig.RDContribDir, 'SA_Score'))
import sascorer                      # not a normal import — it lives in Contrib

for name, smi in [("aspirin",   "CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O"),
                  ("caffeine",  "Cn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C"),
                  ("a generated molecule", "...")]:
    m = Chem.MolFromSmiles(smi)
    print(name, round(sascorer.calculateScore(m), 2))

ROUGH INTERPRETATION
    < 3.0   easy, likely 1-3 steps
    3 - 4   routine
    4 - 5   getting involved
    5 - 6   hard; question whether it is worth it
    > 6     assume not, unless a chemist says otherwise

PRACTICAL FILTER FOR GENERATIVE OUTPUT
    hit finding        SA < 5.0, <= 4 stereocentres, <= 6 rings
    lead optimisation  SA < 4.0, <= 2 stereocentres, <= 4 rings

⭐ AND THE RULE THAT MATTERS MOST
   NEITHER SCORE IS RELIABLE ALONE. They disagree, and they
   disagree in different directions. Use both, and treat them as
   a triage filter that removes the obviously impossible — not
   as a verdict that something IS makeable. Only a route is that.
Lesson 14

Retrosynthesis — working backwards

A score says "this looks hard". A route says "here is how". Retrosynthesis is the chemist's method for finding one, and it runs backwards: take the target, break a bond to get simpler pieces, repeat until everything is something you can buy.

  THE DISCONNECTION IDEA

     TARGET          an amide:   R-C(=O)-NH-R'
        |
        |  disconnect the C-N bond
        v
     PRECURSORS      R-C(=O)-OH   +   H2N-R'
                     an acid          an amine
                         |                |
                    can I buy these?      |
                         |                |
                    yes -> done      no -> disconnect again

  ⭐ WHY BACKWARDS: there are millions of possible molecules you
     could start from, but only a handful of sensible ways to take
     THIS molecule apart. Working backwards makes the search small
     enough to actually do.

  THE THING BEGINNERS MISS
     A "disconnection" is not any bond break. It is a bond break
     that corresponds to a REAL REACTION run in reverse. Amide
     bonds disconnect easily because amide coupling is reliable
     chemistry. Two adjacent quaternary carbons do not disconnect
     at all, because no reliable reaction makes that bond.
Lesson 15

Template-based versus template-free

Template-basedTemplate-free
The ideaA library of reaction rules written as SMARTS, extracted from reaction databases. Match a rule, apply it in reverse.Treat it as translation: feed the product SMILES to a Transformer, get reactant SMILES out.
StrengthEvery proposal corresponds to a documented reaction. Interpretable — you can see which rule fired.Not limited to the rule library; can propose disconnections nobody templated.
WeaknessCannot propose anything outside its templates. Template extraction is fiddly and stereochemistry is easy to get wrong.Can produce confident nonsense. No guarantee the proposed reaction exists.
AccuracyMolecular Transformer (Schwaller et al., 2019) on USPTO-50K: top-1 ≈ 43–47%, top-5 ≈ 65–70%, top-10 ≈ 75–80%.
Why 45% is not as bad as it sounds

Top-1 accuracy is measured against the single route recorded in the patent. Most molecules have several valid routes, so a model can propose a perfectly good synthesis and be scored wrong. For calibration: human experts disagree with each other on the best route roughly 30% of the time. Read top-10 accuracy instead — it is closer to the question you actually care about, which is "is a usable route somewhere in the suggestions?"

Lesson 16

CASP tools, and the question everyone forgets

Computer-Aided Synthesis Planning tools chain single steps into full routes. AiZynthFinder (Genheden et al., 2020, AstraZeneca) is the open-source standard.

  HOW AIZYNTHFINDER WORKS

    1. EXPANSION POLICY   a neural net trained on USPTO proposes
                          plausible one-step disconnections
    2. FILTER POLICY      a second net removes chemically
                          unreasonable proposals
    3. STOCK              the list of molecules you can BUY
    4. MCTS               Monte Carlo tree search explores the
                          tree of routes, balancing promising
                          paths against unexplored ones

    A route is SOLVED when every leaf is in the stock.

  ⭐ THE QUESTION EVERYONE FORGETS: WHAT IS IN YOUR STOCK?

     The default stock is ZINC — roughly 290,000 commercially
     available compounds. So "solved" means "solved, assuming you
     can buy anything in ZINC."

     If your lab buys from two vendors and has an in-house
     compound collection, the default answer is wrong in both
     directions: it proposes starting materials you cannot get,
     and it misses routes through compounds already on your shelf.

     SWAPPING IN YOUR OWN STOCK FILE IS THE SINGLE HIGHEST-VALUE
     THING YOU CAN DO WITH THIS TOOL, and it takes an afternoon.
Lesson 17

Scoring a route — why shorter is often worse

Worked example · three steps versus six 
ROUTE A — 3 steps, all difficult
    asymmetric Ir-catalysed C-H borylation   ~50% yield
    challenging Suzuki coupling              ~50% yield
    sensitive reductive amination            ~50% yield

    overall yield = 0.50 x 0.50 x 0.50 = 0.125  ->  12.5%

ROUTE B — 6 steps, all routine
    six reliable, well-precedented reactions  ~90% each

    overall yield = 0.90 ^ 6 = 0.531           ->  53.1%

⭐ ROUTE B WINS, AND IT IS NOT CLOSE.
   Four times the material from twice the steps.

AND THE ARITHMETIC UNDERSTATES IT, because a "difficult" step
also carries a real chance of not working at all, costing weeks.

THE OTHER THING THE STEP COUNT HIDES: PROTECTING GROUPS.
   A 3-step route on a molecule with both a free amine and an
   acid chloride actually needs protection and deprotection —
   2 extra steps each way. The honest count is 7, not 3, and
   most AI tools will not tell you this.

⭐ SO SCORE ROUTES BY: step reliability, starting material cost
   and availability, protecting-group burden, and scalability —
   not by length.
On reaction data, briefly

Essentially every ML retrosynthesis model is trained on USPTO — reactions text-mined from US patents by Daniel Lowe. USPTO-50K is the standard benchmark, USPTO-Full is around a million reactions. Know its flaws: the extraction is automated so there are parsing errors, atom mapping is often wrong, yields are unreported or flattering because patents are not written to be honest about yield, and coverage is heavily biased toward pharmaceutical chemistry. There is no free alternative with comparable coverage, which is why everyone uses it anyway. The Open Reaction Database is the modern successor and is growing.

One-sentence answers to have ready
How does a SMILES model produce valid molecules?

  its strings do not parse.

What does temperature do?
  Sharpens or flattens the next-character distribution. Low =
  valid and repetitive. High = novel and often broken.

What is sigma in REINVENT?
  The leash to the prior. Standard 128. Too low, the agent drifts
  into nonsense that games the score; too high, nothing optimises.

Why is 100% validity not a good sign?
  Because JT-VAE-style methods guarantee it by construction, so
  the number carries no information about quality.

Your reward went 0.2 -> 0.9. What did you prove?
  That the model maximised my scoring function. Nothing else.
  Whether the molecules are good is a separate question, and the
  score cannot answer it because the score is what was gamed.

How do you spot mode collapse?
  Uniqueness falling while reward rises; mean pairwise Tanimoto
  above ~0.6; a handful of distinct scaffolds from thousands of
  molecules.

SA Score versus SCScore?
  SA: heuristic, 1 easy to 10 hard, fragment frequency in PubChem.
  SC: learned from reactions, 1 simple to 5 complex. They
  disagree; use both, and only a route settles it.

Why is a 6-step route often better than a 3-step one?
  0.9^6 = 53% versus 0.5^3 = 12.5%. Reliability beats brevity,
  and the short route hides protecting-group steps.

What is the first thing to change in AiZynthFinder?
  The stock file. The default is ZINC (~290k); your lab's actual
  availability is a different list, and "solved" is meaningless
  against the wrong one.

Why is USPTO flawed but universal?
  Text-mined from patents: parsing errors, bad atom mapping,
  unreliable yields, pharma bias. There is no free alternative
  with comparable coverage.
