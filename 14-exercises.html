Before you start

How to run one

These are not tutorial exercises

A tutorial exercise says: "load this clean CSV, train a random forest, report accuracy."

These say: "three different CROs ran screens for us over two years and their exports are a mess. The VP wants a predictive model by Thursday. The last contractor used random splits and it failed in prospective validation. Do not repeat that."

The mess is the exercise. What is being tested is whether you notice the trap before you notice the task.

The four rules that make them worth the time
1. READ THE SCENARIO FIRST, AND ANSWER THE STAKEHOLDER'S
   ACTUAL QUESTION.
   "A predictive model for potency" is not a specification.
   Noticing that is half the skill being tested.

2. SPEND TWO HOURS BEFORE LOOKING AT THE SOLUTION.
   The difference between your approach and the solution IS the
   lesson. Reading first destroys it permanently.

3. READ THE MISTAKES SECTION EVEN IF YOU SUCCEEDED.
   You cannot discover all of them by accident in four hours,
   and knowing what not to do is half the job.

4. ⭐ WRITE FOUR SENTENCES AT THE END.
   what the question was · what you did · what the number was ·
   WHAT YOU TRIED THAT DID NOT WORK.

   That last clause is what makes an interview answer sound like
   experience instead of a tutorial — and it only exists if you
   write it down while the work is fresh.

Part I

Building the dataset

Exercise 01 · 5–6 hours

The chemical data curation pipeline

The brief

Three CROs, two years, one new CDK4/6 programme. Their exports disagree about everything. Produce a clean, deduplicated, unit-consistent dataset the modelling team can use — and an audit trail.

What the solution does, in order 
1  PARSE and log failures. Never drop a row silently.
2  STRIP SALTS before anything else. "drug.HCl" and "drug"
   are one compound, and canonicalising first hides that.
3  CANONICALISE, then key on InChIKey — not on the SMILES
   string, which differs for identical molecules.
4  SEPARATE BY ASSAY TYPE. IC50, Ki and EC50 into different
   columns. They are not the same measurement.
5  CONVERT to pIC50 = -log10(molar).
6  AGGREGATE duplicates with the GEOMETRIC mean.
7  FLAG censored values (">10000") rather than casting them.
8  APPLY the purity threshold — and record how many it removed.
9  WRITE THE LOG: every rule, and the row count it touched.
The five mistakes

Deduplicating on SMILES instead of InChIKey · arithmetic instead of geometric mean · canonicalising before stripping salts · mixing activity units · dropping low-purity compounds without saying so.

What a senior checks

The log, before the dataset. Then: how many rows did each rule remove, and does the total reconcile with the input count?

Try it · Three labs measured the same compound: 10 nM, 100 nM and 10,000 nM. What single number goes in your dataset?
Answer

214 nM — the geometric mean, which you get by averaging in pIC50 space.

ARITHMETIC MEAN, in nM
    (10 + 100 + 10000) / 3  =  3,370 nM
    ⚠ one bad measurement has swallowed the other two

GEOMETRIC MEAN, via pIC50
    pIC50 =  8.0,  7.0,  5.0
    mean  =  6.67   ->   214 nM
    ✓ balanced, and it is what "average potency" actually means

The reason is that activity is logarithmically distributed. A tenfold error is the same size of mistake whether it happens at 10 nM or at 10 µM, and only the log scale treats it that way. Arithmetic averaging in nM lets your weakest measurement decide the answer.

And the deeper habit: if three labs disagree by a factor of 1,000, the disagreement is itself the finding. Averaging it away hides the fact that one of those assays is probably not measuring what the others are. Flag it before you average it.

Exercise 02 · 4–5 hours

Molecular descriptor selection

The brief

~3,200 clean compounds from exercise 01. The modelling lead wants a descriptor matrix, and is explicit that dumping 2,000 RDKit descriptors into a CSV is not an acceptable answer — the last person who did that produced a model that memorised the training set.

What the solution does
  • Drop constant and near-constant descriptors — useless on this set regardless of fame.
  • Drop one of each highly correlated pair. MW, heavy-atom count and molar refractivity are close to one descriptor wearing three hats.
  • Scale before any distance-based step, and fit the scaler inside the fold.
  • Justify the surviving set on chemistry, then document every choice.
The trap most people fall into

Selecting descriptors by their correlation with the target. It feels like feature selection and it is leakage: you looked at the answers to choose the questions, and your test score is now optimistic by an amount you cannot measure.

What a senior checks

"Why these descriptors?" A good answer mentions chemistry. A weak one mentions only variance. And: "does molecular weight belong in this model at all?" — often it does not, because it correlates with potency for reasons that have nothing to do with binding.

Part II

Building the model

Exercise 03 · 6–8 hours

QSAR, validated properly

The most important exercise in the course

You are three minutes into presenting your first model when Dr Okoro stops you: "You used random 80/20 splits? Let me tell you what happened at my last company. A junior modeller built a beautiful QSAR model for a JAK2 project. R² = 0.92 on the test set…"

Everything in this exercise is about the gap between a number that is correct and a number that is predictive of your experience.

What the solution requires
  • A scaffold split, with the train/test scaffold overlap printed and equal to zero.
  • Both numbers reported — random and scaffold — and the gap between them named as the deception factor.
  • An applicability domain, with error plotted against distance from the training set.
  • A Ridge baseline, so "the random forest helped" is a measurement rather than an assumption.
  • Hyperparameters tuned inside the training set only.
  • Alignment with the OECD validation principles.
The five mistakes

Random splits reported as the headline · no applicability domain · comparing models on R² alone · tuning on the test set · and a scaffold split that still leaks because the scaffolds were computed after some other grouping.

What a senior checks 
1 "Print the train/test scaffold overlap." If it is > 0,
  the split is wrong, and every number after it is wrong.
2 "Show me the R2 gap." If it is < 0.05, either your
  scaffold diversity is low or you have very few scaffolds —
  so the split is not testing what you think.
3 "Is there a trend between AD distance and error?"
  There SHOULD be. If not, your AD measure is not measuring.
4 "Does RF actually beat Ridge by a meaningful margin?"
  If not, the non-linear SAR is weak: you need more data,
  not a fancier model.
5 "Pick the 10 test compounds the model is most confident
  about. Those are our synthesis priorities." ⭐ The model
  only matters if it changes what gets made.
Try it · Your scaffold split gives R² = 0.41 and the random split gave 0.82. Which do you report, and how do you survive the meeting?
Answer

Report 0.41 as the headline and 0.82 beside it — and do not argue about statistics. Reframe it as a question about what the model is for.

"Both numbers are correct, and they answer different questions. The 0.82 says how well we predict more compounds from series we already know. The 0.41 says how well we predict a new series. Since we are going to use this on new chemistry, 0.41 is the number that predicts our experience."

Then make the cost concrete: "if we report 0.82 and it performs at 0.41, the chemists lose trust in the model and stop using it — and we will not get that back."

And say what the gap tells you, because it is genuinely informative rather than merely embarrassing: a large gap means the model is learning series membership rather than chemistry. That is a finding about your dataset's diversity, and it points at the fix — more scaffolds, not more compounds.

Exercise 04 · 5–6 hours

The ADMET filtering cascade

The brief

50,000 compounds were docked; the top 5,000 arrive on your desk. Docking says nothing about whether these survive in a living system. Build the cascade that takes 5,000 down to something a chemistry team can act on.

The arithmetic that must be in your head 
Six filters, each passing 70%:

    0.70 ^ 6  =  0.118      ->  you keep 12%

Every filter looked reasonable in isolation. Together they
discarded 88% — and because each model has error, a real
fraction of what you discarded was fine.

⭐ ORDER MATTERS: cheap and reliable filters first, expensive
   and uncertain ones last. You want to spend compute only on
   compounds that already survived the certain tests.
The five mistakes

Filters in the wrong order · binary pass/fail instead of ranked scoring · ignoring hERG, which is the one that kills programmes late · not reporting the funnel · optimising for potency alone.

The senior habit

Flag and rank rather than hard-filter. A compound failing one soft criterion should be ranked lower, not deleted — a hard cutoff applied to an uncertain prediction throws away real molecules and leaves no record that it happened. And always hand over the funnel with counts at every stage.

Part III

Using it

Exercise 05 · 6–8 hours

Virtual screening triage

The brief

A CDK4/6 co-crystal structure at 1.9 Å has just been solved, and there is a commercial agreement for 100,000 purchasable compounds with three-week delivery. The head of screening wants to move fast. Design the campaign.

What the solution does
  • Uses several query molecules, not one — a single query finds a single neighbourhood.
  • Uses more than one fingerprint type, because they disagree about what "similar" means.
  • Clusters before selecting, so the shortlist is not a hundred costumes on one molecule.
  • Checks purchasability and actual availability before anything is proposed.
  • Visualises chemical space coverage to show what the screen did not reach.
The deliverable

A shortlist with a one-line rationale per compound, a funnel with counts, and a coverage plot. The funnel is what gets you asked back.

Exercise 06 · 4–5 hours

Activity cliff analysis

The brief

A medicinal chemist has spent three months making 80 analogues of your best hit, and the SAR "makes no sense". Explain it. This is the exercise where a cheminformatician is most obviously useful to a chemist, and it is mostly a communication task.

The method 
SALI — Structure-Activity Landscape Index

    SALI(i,j) = | pIC50_i - pIC50_j |  /  ( 1 - sim(i,j) )
                 ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^      ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
                 how different the        how SIMILAR the
                 activity is              structures are

  Both big  ->  a cliff. Near-identical molecules, wildly
                different potency.

  ⚠ THE DENOMINATOR EXPLODES as similarity approaches 1, so
    two nearly identical molecules give an enormous SALI from
    a small activity difference. Normalise, and set a minimum
    activity difference before you believe any of it.
The five mistakes

Euclidean distance instead of Tanimoto · unnormalised SALI · mistaking assay noise for a cliff — a 3-fold difference inside 2-fold assay error is nothing · reporting cliffs with no med-chem guidance · ignoring stereochemistry, where the "identical" pair is actually two different molecules.

The communication test

"Replacing the meta-fluorine with a chlorine costs 50-fold, and here are three more pairs showing the same thing — that pocket is small" is useful. "Tanimoto 0.94 with ΔpIC50 1.7" is not. Same finding, and only one of them changes what gets synthesised.

Try it · You find a pair at Tanimoto 0.97 with a 4-fold potency difference and a huge SALI. Is it a cliff?
Answer

Almost certainly not. It is probably assay noise with a small denominator.

Two checks settle it:

1  WHAT IS THE ASSAY'S OWN VARIABILITY?
   Most cell-based IC50 assays repeat to within 2-3 fold.
   A 4-fold difference is barely outside the noise, and a
   single measurement each side is not evidence.

2  WHAT IS THE DENOMINATOR DOING?
   sim = 0.97  ->  1 - sim = 0.03
   SALI = 0.6 / 0.03 = 20      <- looks dramatic
   The number is large because you divided by 0.03, not
   because the chemistry is interesting.

The rule: set a minimum activity difference — conventionally around 10-fold, or one pIC50 unit — before a pair is allowed to be called a cliff at all, and check it has been measured more than once. Then rank the survivors by SALI.

Reporting noise as a cliff is worse than reporting nothing, because a chemist may go and make compounds based on it.

Exercise 07 · 5–7 hours

Chemical series profiling

The brief

Eighteen months in, four series — pyrimidine, quinazoline, benzimidazole, indole — with 50–80 compounds each, every one championed by a different chemist. The project leader needs a selection decision. You are being asked to help end somebody's project, which is why the presentation matters as much as the analysis.

The five mistakes

Comparing means without looking at distributions · ignoring within-series variance · the arithmetic mean of pIC50 again · ignoring correlations between properties · and making a recommendation without stating the trade-offs explicitly.

What the analysis must show

The distributions, not the averages — a series with a lower mean but a much better best compound is often the right bet, and a mean hides that entirely. Then multi-parameter profiles across potency, ADMET and synthesisability, with the trade-offs named.

The artefact

A running Streamlit dashboard with structures drawn, applicability-domain flags on every prediction, and export to the format the team already uses. This is the most portfolio-friendly exercise in the set, because it is the only one a non-specialist can see working.

Part IV

When it breaks

Exercise 08 · 4–5 hours

The model failure investigation

The exercise closest to a senior person's actual week

Six months ago you deployed the CDK4/6 model — random forest, scaffold split, RMSE 0.6, considered reliable. The team has been using it to prioritise synthesis. This morning the head of medicinal chemistry emails: "We have a serious problem."

No new model is required. This is debugging.

The order that finds it fastest 
1 HAS THE INPUT CHANGED?
  Applicability-domain coverage of recent submissions versus
  training. ⭐ Fastest signal, and usually the answer.
2 HAS THE SCAFFOLD SHIFTED?
  Did the project move from hit finding into lead
  optimisation, or start a new series?
3 HAS THE ASSAY CHANGED?
  New cell line or readout — the labels moved, not the
  molecules.
4 HAS THE PIPELINE CHANGED?
  An RDKit version bump can alter descriptors silently.
5 ONLY THEN suspect the model itself.
The answer, in this case

A triazine scaffold that was not in the training data at all. The model was never wrong about the chemistry it knew; it was asked about chemistry it had never seen, and it answered anyway — because that is what a model without an enforced applicability domain does.

The five mistakes

Blaming the model before checking the data · not verifying the experimental results are themselves correct · retraining before understanding the failure · having no applicability-domain monitoring in production · and blaming the users.

The artefact

A root-cause document: symptom, cause, evidence, fix, and what monitoring would have caught it weeks earlier. That last section is the one that demonstrates seniority, and it is the one most people leave out.

Try it · The deployed model's RMSE has doubled. Before you look at the model, what three numbers do you pull?
Answer 
1  AD COVERAGE OF RECENT SUBMISSIONS
   What fraction of the last 60 days' predictions fell
   INSIDE the training distribution? If that has collapsed,
   you have your answer and the model is blameless.

2  SCAFFOLD NOVELTY RATE
   How many recent compounds carry a Murcko scaffold absent
   from training? A new series entering the programme is the
   single most common cause.

3  ERROR SPLIT: INSIDE AD vs OUTSIDE AD
   If inside-AD error is unchanged and outside-AD error
   exploded, the model still works — it is being MISUSED,
   which is a governance fix rather than a modelling one.

All three are about the inputs, not the model, and that ordering is the whole lesson. A model does not degrade on its own; the world it is being asked about moves.

The fourth thing to check, before retraining anything: are the new experimental numbers themselves right? A changed assay protocol produces exactly this symptom, and retraining on bad labels makes it permanent.

Part V

The whole thing at once

Exercise 09 · the end-to-end project

A reaction intelligence platform

The one that consolidates everything

Build a platform over organic reaction data: parse and store reactions, classify them against named-reaction SMARTS templates, predict outcomes, and serve it. It touches every volume — representations, RDKit, descriptors, curation, modelling, retrosynthesis and the service layer.

Do it in thin vertical slices. One reaction class parsed, classified, predicted and served end to end before you start the second. The temptation is to perfect the parser first; resist it, because you only learn what the parser needs by having something downstream that consumes its output.

The mistakes that recur across exercises

Forty-odd mistakes are catalogued across the nine briefs. These are the ones that appear in more than one, which makes them habits rather than slips.

The habitAppears inWhy it keeps happening
Arithmetic mean of a log quantity01, 07Averaging pIC50 or IC50 without thinking about the scale. Activity is logarithmic; arithmetic means let the weakest measurement decide.
Looking at the target before splitting02, 03Feature selection, scaling and imputation done on the full dataset. It feels like preparation and it is leakage.
No applicability domain03, 04, 08The model answers every question it is asked. Without an AD, nothing distinguishes an answer from an extrapolation.
Hard filters on uncertain predictions04, 05Each filter looks reasonable; the product of six of them discards most of your library, including real compounds.
Top-N without clustering05, 07The top 100 by any score are usually near-duplicates. You buy one molecule a hundred times.
Reporting numbers, not decisions06, 07, 08Tanimoto and ΔpIC50 are not sentences a chemist can act on. The analysis is only finished when it names what to do differently.
Blaming the model03, 08Almost always the data, the split, the units or the inputs. Suspect the model last.
