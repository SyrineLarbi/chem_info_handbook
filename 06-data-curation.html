AI cheminformatics · Volume 06 of 17 · phase 1

Chemical data, and its curation. The volume that decides whether your model means anything.

Public bioactivity data is assembled from thousands of papers, measured in different labs, under different conditions, reported in different units, with the same compound drawn several ways. Cleaning it is not preparatory work before the real modelling — on most projects it is the work, and it is where the largest gains are available.

The databases

Check the licence before you build on it

ChEMBL is CC BY-SA; PubChem is largely open; DrugBank and several others restrict commercial use, and some vendor catalogues have terms that prohibit redistribution. If the model might ever ship in a product, establish this in week one rather than week fifty.

Lesson 02

Pulling data from ChEMBL

Worked example · every measured inhibitor of one target 
from chembl_webresource_client.new_client import new_client

# 1. find the target — always check what you actually matched
targets = new_client.target.filter(pref_name__icontains="acetylcholinesterase")
for t in targets[:5]:
    print(t["target_chembl_id"], t["organism"], t["target_type"])

  CHEMBL220   Homo sapiens        SINGLE PROTEIN     <-- the one you want
  CHEMBL4780  Electrophorus ...   SINGLE PROTEIN     <-- an eel. Not the one you want.
  CHEMBL2095233 ...               PROTEIN FAMILY     <-- a family, not a protein

# 2. pull the activities, restricting hard
acts = new_client.activity.filter(
        target_chembl_id="CHEMBL220",
        standard_type="IC50",           # ONE endpoint type only
        standard_relation="=",          # exclude ">" and "<" censored values
        assay_type="B",                 # B = binding (F = functional, A = ADMET)
).only(["molecule_chembl_id", "canonical_smiles",
        "standard_value", "standard_units",
        "assay_chembl_id", "pchembl_value"])

df = pd.DataFrame(acts)
print(len(df), "activities")

Every one of those filters is load-bearing, and leaving any of them out is a normal beginner mistake:

  • The organism. Mixing human and eel enzyme data trains a model on two different proteins. The name matched both.
  • standard_type. IC₅₀, Kᵢ and EC₅₀ are different quantities. Pooling them puts unrelated numbers in one column.
  • standard_relation="=". Rows reading > 10000 mean "we stopped measuring" — see Lesson 05.
  • assay_type. A binding assay and a cell-based functional assay measure different things and are not interchangeable.

pchembl_value is ChEMBL's own pre-computed −log₁₀ value, which saves you the conversion when it is present.

Part II

Reading activity data

Lesson 03

What a measurement actually means

The endpoints, and what each is measuring
IC₅₀
The concentration that inhibits 50% of the activity. Depends on the assay conditions — notably the substrate concentration — so two labs can legitimately report different values for the same compound.
Kᵢ
The binding constant. A more fundamental quantity, corrected for assay conditions. More comparable across labs, and preferable when available.
EC₅₀
The concentration producing 50% of maximal effect, usually in cells. Includes whether the compound gets into the cell at all, so it is a harder and more realistic test.
Kd
The dissociation constant from a direct binding measurement. Physically clean.
% inhibition
A single-concentration screening result. Not a potency — do not mix it into a potency column, ever.
The rule that follows

One model, one endpoint type, one assay type, one organism. Every relaxation of that is a decision you must be able to defend. "We pooled IC₅₀ and Kᵢ to get more data" is a sentence that ends credibility in a review, because the model is now predicting an average of two different physical quantities.

Lesson 04

pIC₅₀ — the conversion the field always does

Worked example · why nobody models raw IC₅₀ 
pIC50 = -log10( IC50 in MOLAR )

and because data usually arrives in nanomolar:
pIC50 = 9 - log10( IC50 in nM )

  IC50 = 1 nM        -> pIC50 = 9.0     very potent
  IC50 = 10 nM       -> pIC50 = 8.0
  IC50 = 100 nM      -> pIC50 = 7.0
  IC50 = 1000 nM     -> pIC50 = 6.0     (= 1 uM)
  IC50 = 10000 nM    -> pIC50 = 5.0     weak

import numpy as np
df["pIC50"] = 9 - np.log10(df["standard_value"])   # value in nM

Three reasons this conversion is not optional:

  1. It fixes the direction. Raw IC₅₀ is "lower is better", which inverts every intuition about a regression target. pIC₅₀ is "higher is better", like every other target you have modelled.
  2. It fixes the distribution. Raw values span 1 to 10,000,000 and are wildly skewed. A squared-error loss on that is dominated entirely by the weakest compounds — the ones you care least about. On the log scale the distribution is roughly normal.
  3. It matches the underlying physics. Binding free energy is proportional to the log of the constant. So the log scale is the one where the quantity is linear in the thing that actually varies, and where "equal differences mean equal chemistry".

Rule of thumb: a difference of 1 pIC₅₀ unit is a ten-fold potency change, and is a substantial medicinal chemistry result. A model with an error of ±1 log unit is roughly as wrong as it can be while still being useful.

Lesson 05

The seven ways an activity table lies

#The problemHow to detect and handle it
1Mixed units — nM, µM and M in one columnGroup by standard_units and count. Convert everything to one unit before any arithmetic. A model trained across mixed units learns the unit, not the chemistry.
2Censored values — > 10000 meaning "inactive at the highest concentration tested"Check standard_relation. These are not measurements. Either drop them, or keep them as a censored class in a classification model — never treat 10000 as a number.
3Duplicates with different valuesGroup by canonical SMILES. Look at the spread. See Lesson 08 — the spread itself is information.
4Mixed assays — measurements from 40 different papersCount activities per assay_chembl_id. If one assay dominates, your model may be learning that assay. If forty contribute one point each, between-lab variation is now your noise floor.
5Publication biasNobody publishes the 900 inactive analogues. Your "inactives" are mostly weak actives, and the true inactive space is unsampled. This limits what the model can be used for and cannot be fixed by cleaning.
6Transcription errorsValues off by a factor of 1,000 from unit confusion at data entry. Detect by plotting the distribution and inspecting anything more than ~3 log units from its series' median.
7Stereochemistry inconsistencyThe same compound with and without stereo, as different rows. Volume 03, Lesson 04 — decide, apply consistently, record.
Try it · Your target has 1,400 activities. Grouping by assay_chembl_id, one assay contributes 900 and the rest contribute 1–12 each. Is that good or bad?
Answer

Both, and you must say which you are choosing.

Good: those 900 are internally consistent — same lab, same protocol, same conditions — so the noise floor within them is low. That is the cleanest signal you will get.

Bad: 900 compounds from one paper are almost certainly one chemical series. Your model will be excellent at that series and untested anywhere else, and a random split will hide this completely. The applicability domain is far narrower than "1,400 compounds" suggests.

What to actually do: report the model's performance separately on the big assay and on the rest. If it performs well on the 900 and poorly on the others, you have learned the real thing: this is a series model, not a target model — which is still useful, but only if everyone knows it.

Part III

The pipeline

Lesson 06

The order of operations

The order matters, and getting it wrong produces subtly wrong data rather than an error. Deduplicating before standardising, for instance, leaves duplicates behind, because the standardisation is what makes them identical.

  RAW DOWNLOAD                        never edited, kept forever
       |
   1.  PARSE                          SMILES -> Mol.  Log and count failures.
       |
   2.  FILTER TO ONE ENDPOINT         one standard_type, one assay_type, one organism
       |
   3.  DROP CENSORED                  standard_relation must be "="
       |
   4.  UNIFY UNITS                    everything to nM, then to pIC50
       |
   5.  STANDARDISE STRUCTURE          desalt -> neutralise -> canonical tautomer
       |                              ** must come BEFORE dedup **
   6.  CANONICALISE SMILES            the key you will group on
       |
   7.  DEDUPLICATE + AGGREGATE        group by canonical SMILES; decide what to do
       |                              with disagreeing values
   8.  FILTER STRUCTURES              PAINS, reactives, size bounds, element whitelist
       |
   9.  RECORD                         counts at every step, in a file, versioned
       |
       v
  MODEL-READY SET

  ⭐ AT EVERY ARROW, WRITE DOWN HOW MANY ROWS SURVIVED.
     That table of counts is the single most useful artefact of the
     whole pipeline, and the first thing a reviewer will ask for.
Lesson 07

Standardising structures

Worked example · the standardisation function 
from rdkit import Chem
from rdkit.Chem.MolStandardize import rdMolStandardize

_largest   = rdMolStandardize.LargestFragmentChooser()
_uncharger = rdMolStandardize.Uncharger()
_taut      = rdMolStandardize.TautomerEnumerator()

def standardise(smiles):
    mol = Chem.MolFromSmiles(smiles)
    if mol is None:
        return None
    mol = rdMolStandardize.Cleanup(mol)      # sanitise, fix common drawing issues
    mol = _largest.choose(mol)               # drop counterions / solvents
    mol = _uncharger.uncharge(mol)           # neutralise where chemically sensible
    mol = _taut.Canonicalize(mol)            # ONE tautomer, consistently
    return Chem.MolToSmiles(mol)             # canonical SMILES out

# always measure the effect:
df["std_smiles"] = df["canonical_smiles"].apply(standardise)
changed = (df["std_smiles"] != df["canonical_smiles"]).sum()
print(f"{changed} of {len(df)} structures were altered")

That last measurement is the part people skip. If standardisation changed 3% of rows, fine. If it changed 60%, something systematic is happening — the source stores everything as a salt, or your tautomer step is aggressive — and you want to look at twenty examples by eye before proceeding.

Note also that tautomer canonicalisation is slow — it enumerates possible forms. On a million compounds it is the bottleneck, and the usual answer is to cache results keyed on the input SMILES.

Lesson 08

Deduplicating — and what to do when values disagree

After standardisation, group by canonical SMILES. Some compounds will appear several times with different measured values. This is the most consequential judgement call in the pipeline.

First: look at the spread, do not act yet 
g = df.groupby("std_smiles")["pIC50"]
spread = (g.max() - g.min())
print(spread.describe())
print((spread > 1).sum(), "compounds disagree by more than 10-fold")

That number is your experimental noise floor. A model cannot be more accurate than its labels are consistent — so if 20% of compounds disagree by more than 1 log unit, an RMSE of 0.3 is not achievable and anyone reporting it has leaked.

Then choose, by how far apart they are
  • Spread < 1 log unit → take the median. Robust to a single bad value, unlike the mean.
  • Spread > 1 log unit → drop the compound. You do not know which value is right, and a wrong label is worse than a missing one.
  • Spread huge (> 2 units) → inspect. This is often two different assays, or a data error, and is worth understanding rather than discarding silently.
What to record

Keep a column with the number of measurements and the spread for every retained compound. It lets you weight training by confidence later, and it lets you explain a bad prediction by pointing at a label that was never reliable.

Lesson 09

Filtering the junk

FilterRemovesThe caution
PAINSSubstructures notorious for producing false positives in assays — they interfere with the readout rather than binding the target.Published as ~480 SMARTS patterns, available in RDKit's FilterCatalog. Flag rather than delete: several approved drugs contain PAINS substructures, and the filters were derived from one assay technology.
Reactive groupsAcyl halides, epoxides, Michael acceptors — things that react with the protein non-specifically.Covalent drugs are a deliberate and successful class. Filter for a screening library; do not assume reactive means bad.
Size boundsAnything under ~6 or over ~70 heavy atoms.Usually catches fragments and polymers that crept in. Safe.
Element whitelistAnything containing metals or unusual elements.Fingerprints and descriptors handle organics; a platinum complex will be represented badly by both. Restrict to the common organic set unless the project is about metals.
AggregatorsCompounds that clump into colloids and inhibit non-specifically.Hard to detect structurally. Mostly handled experimentally, but worth knowing as an explanation for weird actives.
Running the PAINS filter
from rdkit.Chem import FilterCatalog

params = FilterCatalog.FilterCatalogParams()
params.AddCatalog(FilterCatalog.FilterCatalogParams.FilterCatalogs.PAINS)
catalog = FilterCatalog.FilterCatalog(params)

df["pains"] = df["mol"].apply(lambda m: catalog.HasMatch(m))
print(df["pains"].sum(), "compounds match a PAINS pattern")
# KEEP the column. Do not drop the rows without looking at them.
Part IV

Judgement

Lesson 10

When not to clean

Three cases where cleaning destroys the finding
  • The outlier is an activity cliff. A compound whose activity is wildly out of line with its neighbours may be the most chemically interesting record you have. Removing "outliers" by a statistical rule deletes exactly the compounds a medicinal chemist most wants to understand.
  • The duplicates disagree because the assays disagree. That is a finding about the assays, and it belongs in your report rather than being averaged away.
  • The missing data is not missing at random. Compounds lacking a solubility measurement are often those that were too insoluble to measure. Dropping them makes your dataset systematically more soluble than reality, and your model will be optimistic in precisely the region where it matters.

The discipline: every removal is a decision with a reason, applied by a rule you wrote down, never by eye and never by a blanket statistical threshold.

Lesson 11

The audit trail

Worked example · the artefact to produce every time 
CURATION REPORT — target CHEMBL220, generated 2026-09-12
source: ChEMBL 35 · RDKit 2025.03.1

  raw activities downloaded                       4,812
  after endpoint filter (IC50, binding, human)    2,140   -2,672
  after dropping censored (relation != "=")       1,905     -235
  after unit unification (nM)                     1,905        0
  parsed successfully                             1,898       -7
  after structure standardisation                 1,898        0   (412 altered)
  unique compounds after canonicalisation         1,247     -651   (duplicates merged)
    of which had >1 measurement                     398
    dropped: spread > 1 log unit                     54
  final, after structure filters                  1,173      -20
    flagged PAINS (kept, flagged)                    31

  FINAL TRAINING SET: 1,173 compounds
  pIC50 range 4.1 - 10.2, median 6.8
  noise floor (median spread of replicates): 0.31 log units

This table is the deliverable. It tells a reader how much data there really was, what was thrown away and why, and — via the noise floor — what model performance is even achievable. Produce it automatically as part of the pipeline, commit it beside the dataset, and you will never have to reconstruct the reasoning from memory.
