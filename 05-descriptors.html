Part I

Descriptors

Lesson 01

What a descriptor is

A descriptor is one number computed from a structure. That is all. Molecular weight is a descriptor. So is the number of rings, the count of nitrogen atoms, and an estimate of how greasy the molecule is.

RDKit ships around 200 of them. You will use about eight regularly, and the temptation to throw all 200 at a model is one this volume will argue you out of.

FamilyExamplesWhat they are for
ConstitutionalMW, atom counts, ring count, heavy atomsCrude size and composition. Cheap and interpretable.
PhysicochemicalLogP, TPSA, HBD, HBAProxies for how the molecule behaves in water and in fat — which is what governs absorption.
TopologicalConnectivity indices, Balaban J, Kappa shapeNumbers summarising the shape of the graph. Historically important, hard to interpret.
ElectronicPartial charges, Gasteiger chargesWhere the molecule is electron-rich. Approximate without quantum chemistry.
3DRadius of gyration, PMI, solvent-accessible surfaceRequire a conformer, so they inherit the conformer problem from Volume 03.
Lesson 02

The eight that matter

Worked example · profiling the course molecules 
from rdkit import Chem
from rdkit.Chem import Descriptors, Crippen, rdMolDescriptors as rdmd

def profile(smi):
    m = Chem.MolFromSmiles(smi)
    return {
      "MW":       round(Descriptors.MolWt(m), 1),        # size
      "LogP":     round(Crippen.MolLogP(m), 2),          # greasiness (calculated)
      "TPSA":     round(rdmd.CalcTPSA(m), 1),            # polar surface area
      "HBD":      rdmd.CalcNumHBD(m),                    # H-bond DONORS
      "HBA":      rdmd.CalcNumHBA(m),                    # H-bond ACCEPTORS
      "RotB":     rdmd.CalcNumRotatableBonds(m),         # flexibility
      "Rings":    rdmd.CalcNumRings(m),
      "HeavyAt":  m.GetNumHeavyAtoms(),
    }

  aspirin      MW 180.2  LogP  1.31  TPSA  63.6  HBD 1  HBA 3  RotB 2  Rings 1
  paracetamol  MW 151.2  LogP  1.35  TPSA  49.3  HBD 2  HBA 2  RotB 1  Rings 1
  caffeine     MW 194.2  LogP -1.03  TPSA  58.4  HBD 0  HBA 3  RotB 0  Rings 2

(values are RDKit's; other toolkits differ slightly — see the warning below)

Read the caffeine row. Its LogP is negative — it prefers water to fat — and it has zero rotatable bonds, because its two fused rings and three methyls leave nothing free to turn. That combination of rigid and water-loving is exactly why caffeine crosses into the brain quickly and is cleared quickly. The descriptors are telling you something real about the substance, and learning to read them that way is the skill.

What each one is a proxy for
LogP
How the molecule splits between oil and water. Higher = greasier. Too low and it will not cross a membrane; too high and it will not dissolve, and it starts binding to things it should not. It is calculated, not measured — the measured quantity is logP and the estimate is usually written cLogP.
TPSA
Total polar surface area, summed from the polar atoms. A remarkably good proxy for whether a molecule can cross a membrane. Below ~140 Å² for oral absorption; below ~90 Å² to have a chance of reaching the brain.
HBD / HBA
Hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors. Every hydrogen bond a molecule makes with water is one it must break to enter a membrane — so more of them means harder absorption.
Rotatable bonds
Flexibility. A floppy molecule pays a bigger entropy cost to lock into a binding site, and tends to be orally absorbed less well.
Descriptors are not universal constants

LogP from RDKit is not LogP from another toolkit. They use different atom-typing schemes and can differ by half a unit or more. HBD and HBA counts differ too, because "what counts as an acceptor" is a definition, not a fact. So: never mix descriptor values from two sources in one dataset, and always record which implementation produced them.

Lesson 03

Lipinski's Rule of Five, properly understood

The most cited — and most misused — heuristic in the field. Here it is, then here is what it actually means.

The rule itself
POOR ORAL ABSORPTION IS MORE LIKELY WHEN TWO OR MORE OF THESE ARE TRUE:

    MW   > 500 Da
    LogP >   5
    HBD  >   5
    HBA  >  10

⭐ IT IS CALLED "RULE OF FIVE" BECAUSE EVERY THRESHOLD IS A MULTIPLE OF 5,
   NOT BECAUSE THERE ARE FIVE RULES. There are four.

  aspirin      MW 180  LogP 1.31  HBD 1  HBA 3    -> 0 violations
  paracetamol  MW 151  LogP 1.35  HBD 2  HBA 2    -> 0 violations
  caffeine     MW 194  LogP -1.03 HBD 0  HBA 3    -> 0 violations
What it actually says

Lipinski analysed compounds that had reached Phase II clinical trials and noticed they clustered inside those bounds. It is an observation about oral bioavailability — whether a pill survives the gut and gets into the blood.

The first thing everyone gets wrong

It says nothing about whether a molecule is active. A compound can pass all four and do nothing at all; a compound can fail all four and be a superb drug. Using Ro5 to filter a screening library for activity is a category error, and it is common.

The second thing everyone gets wrong

It only applies to orally administered small molecules. Injectables are exempt. So are antibiotics, antifungals, vitamins and most natural products — Lipinski said so in the original paper. Roughly a sixth of marketed oral drugs violate it anyway.

And it is irrelevant to the whole modern class of larger molecules — peptides, PROTACs — where people now speak of "beyond Rule of Five" space deliberately.

How to use it correctly

As a flag, not a filter. Compute the violation count, keep it as a column, sort by it, look at what is extreme. Do not delete rows with it. The one-sentence version to have ready: "Ro5 is a statistical observation about oral bioavailability from 1997, not a law, and it is a reason to look twice rather than a reason to discard."

Try it · A colleague filters a 2-million-compound virtual screening library down to 1.4M by removing all Ro5 violators, before docking. Give two reasons this may have been a mistake.
Answer
  1. The target may not need an oral drug. If the programme is aiming at an injectable, an inhaled therapy, or a topical one, oral bioavailability is not a constraint and 600,000 compounds were discarded for a property nobody needed.
  2. Screening hits are meant to be small and weak. Hit-to-lead optimisation adds molecular weight — typically a lot of it. Filtering the starting library at MW ≤ 500 means anything you improve will breach the limit. Screening libraries are usually filtered more tightly than Ro5 and in the other direction: "lead-like" criteria of roughly MW ≤ 350 and LogP ≤ 3 leave room to grow.

The deeper point: a filter applied at the wrong stage removes compounds whose value was in what they would become. And because false negatives are invisible (Volume 01, Lesson 06), nobody will ever know.

Part II

Fingerprints

Lesson 05

The idea

Descriptors summarise a molecule into a handful of numbers, and in doing so throw away the structure. A fingerprint keeps the structure by asking a long list of yes/no questions:

  A FINGERPRINT IS A LONG VECTOR OF BITS. EACH BIT ANSWERS:
      "does this molecule contain THIS particular fragment?"

  molecule ---> [0,1,1,0,0,0,1,0,1,0, ... ] 2048 bits

  bit 41   = "contains a carbon bonded to an aromatic ring"       -> 1
  bit 812  = "contains a carbonyl next to an oxygen"              -> 1
  bit 1337 = "contains a nitrogen in a five-membered ring"        -> 0
  ...

  ⭐ TWO MOLECULES ARE SIMILAR IF THEY SWITCH ON THE SAME BITS.
     That single idea is the basis of virtual screening, clustering,
     diversity selection, and most of Volume 09.
Lesson 06

How a Morgan fingerprint is built

Morgan (also called circular, or ECFP) fingerprints are the default in modern practice. The algorithm is worth understanding because it tells you exactly what the fingerprint can and cannot see.

Worked example · the algorithm, on ethanol 
MOLECULE: CCO        atoms:  0=C   1=C   2=O

ROUND 0 — describe each atom by ITSELF
  atom 0: "carbon, 1 neighbour, 3 H"        -> hash -> 2246728737
  atom 1: "carbon, 2 neighbours, 2 H"       -> hash -> 3542456614
  atom 2: "oxygen, 1 neighbour, 1 H"        -> hash ->  864662311

ROUND 1 (radius 1) — redescribe each atom by ITSELF + ITS NEIGHBOURS
  atom 0: (its own hash) + (atom 1's hash)              -> new hash
  atom 1: (its own hash) + (atoms 0 and 2's hashes)     -> new hash
  atom 2: (its own hash) + (atom 1's hash)              -> new hash

ROUND 2 (radius 2) — again, now reaching two bonds away
  ... each atom's identifier now encodes its whole 2-bond neighbourhood

COLLECT every identifier produced in every round.
FOLD them into a fixed length (e.g. 2048) by taking hash % 2048.
SET those bits to 1.

⭐ SO EACH BIT MEANS: "some atom in this molecule has THIS exact
   circular neighbourhood out to radius r."

Three consequences fall straight out of that description, and they explain most fingerprint behaviour:

  • It is local. With radius 2, no bit encodes anything about atoms more than two bonds apart. Two molecules identical locally but arranged completely differently overall can look similar.
  • It is a set, not a sequence. Nothing records where in the molecule a fragment was, or how many times it occurred — unless you use the count version (Lesson 09).
  • It is sparse and high-dimensional. A typical drug-sized molecule sets perhaps 50–90 of 2048 bits. Most of the vector is zero, which is why bit-vector similarity measures rather than Euclidean distance are used.
Computing one, in practice
from rdkit.Chem import rdFingerprintGenerator

# the current API — the older AllChem.GetMorganFingerprintAsBitVect is deprecated
gen = rdFingerprintGenerator.GetMorganGenerator(radius=2, fpSize=2048)

fp = gen.GetFingerprint(mol)                # an explicit bit vector
arr = gen.GetFingerprintAsNumPy(mol)        # a NumPy array, for sklearn

print(fp.GetNumOnBits())                    # aspirin: ~30 bits set
Lesson 07

The naming trap — ECFP4 is radius 2

This catches everyone exactly once

ECFP4 means diameter 4, which is radius 2. ECFP6 means diameter 6, radius 3. The number in the name is twice the radius you pass to RDKit.

  PAPER SAYS        YOU PASS
  ECFP4             radius=2       <-- the common default
  ECFP6             radius=3
  ECFP2             radius=1

  A paper reporting ECFP4 that you reproduce with radius=4 is
  reproducing something else entirely — and it will not error,
  it will just give worse results you cannot explain.

FCFP is the same algorithm with atoms typed by function (donor, acceptor, aromatic) rather than element — more tolerant of substitutions, so slightly fuzzier and sometimes better for scaffold hopping.

Lesson 08

The fingerprint families

FingerprintEncodesLengthUse it when

The practical advice

Start with Morgan radius 2 at 2048 bits. It is the default for a reason, and beating it takes evidence. If a project matters, benchmark two or three fingerprints on your data with a scaffold split — the ranking between them is dataset-dependent and cannot be read off a paper. Combining Morgan with a handful of physicochemical descriptors is a strong, cheap baseline and is what Volume 08 builds.

Lesson 09

Collisions, and bits versus counts

What a collision is

The algorithm generates far more distinct fragment identifiers than you have bits. Folding them with hash % 2048 means different fragments land on the same bit. Bit 1337 might mean "this nitrogen environment" or "that ester environment", with no way to tell which.

Why it usually does not matter

Because you are comparing whole patterns of ~60 set bits, not individual bits. A few ambiguous positions wash out. Models tolerate it; similarity search tolerates it.

When it does matter

Interpretation. If you ask "which bit drove this prediction?" and map it back to a substructure, a collided bit gives you a confident and wrong answer. If interpretability is the goal, use unfolded/sparse fingerprints, or MACCS where every bit has a published meaning.

Bits versus counts

A bit vector says "present or not". A count vector says how many times. For a molecule with six methyl groups versus one, that difference is real information.

Counts often help tree-based models slightly, and cost nothing: gen.GetCountFingerprintAsNumPy(mol). They matter more for physical properties like solubility — where "how much of this group" is meaningful — than for binding, where presence usually dominates.

Lesson 10

Tanimoto and its relatives

Worked example · how similar are our molecules? 
from rdkit import DataStructs
from rdkit.Chem import rdFingerprintGenerator

gen = rdFingerprintGenerator.GetMorganGenerator(radius=2, fpSize=2048)
fps = {n: gen.GetFingerprint(Chem.MolFromSmiles(s)) for n, s in mols.items()}

DataStructs.TanimotoSimilarity(fps["aspirin"], fps["paracetamol"])   # ~0.22
DataStructs.TanimotoSimilarity(fps["aspirin"], fps["caffeine"])      # ~0.07
DataStructs.TanimotoSimilarity(fps["aspirin"], fps["aspirin"])       # 1.00

# bulk — one query against thousands, in C++
sims = DataStructs.BulkTanimotoSimilarity(query_fp, list_of_fps)

Aspirin and paracetamol score only 0.22 — despite both being small benzene-ring painkillers that a chemist would call obviously related. This is the single most useful number in this volume, because it calibrates your expectations: Tanimoto values on ECFP are much lower than intuition suggests. 0.22 is not "unrelated". It is "recognisably different molecules".

Use BulkTanimotoSimilarity for anything at scale — it loops in C++ rather than Python and is the difference between seconds and hours on a million-compound library.

The three you will meet
Tanimoto (Jaccard)
shared ÷ (in either). The default. Penalises size differences — a small molecule and a large one containing it score low.
Dice
2 × shared ÷ (total in both). More forgiving of size differences. Always ≥ Tanimoto.
Cosine / Tversky
Tversky is asymmetric and has tunable weights — the right choice for "is this small fragment contained in that large molecule?", where Tanimoto is misleading.
Lesson 11

The 0.85 myth

A number repeated far past its evidence

You will read that "Tanimoto ≥ 0.85 means similar". It appears in papers, tutorials and code comments. It comes from work in the 1990s on a specific fingerprint (MDL-style keys) and a specific dataset, and it does not transfer.

On ECFP4, 0.85 is an extremely high bar — usually close analogues of the same compound. Reasonable working thresholds on ECFP4 are more like 0.4–0.5 for "structurally related", and even that is dataset-dependent.

What to do instead: calibrate on your own data. Take 1,000 random pairs from your library, plot the distribution of similarities, and see where your data actually sits. Then pick a threshold you can defend with that plot — and state the fingerprint and its parameters whenever you quote a similarity, because the number is meaningless without them.

Lesson 12

The similarity principle — the assumption underneath everything

The principle

Structurally similar molecules tend to have similar properties. Every similarity search, every QSAR model, and every nearest-neighbour prediction in this field rests on it.

Why it is mostly true

Because binding is physical. A molecule fits a pocket because of its shape and the groups on its surface — so a molecule with nearly the same shape and groups usually fits similarly.

Where it fails: activity cliffs

Two molecules differing by one atom, with a hundred-fold difference in activity. Add a methyl group in the wrong place and it collides with the protein; move a hydrogen-bond donor one position and the whole interaction network breaks.

These are not rare edge cases. They are concentrated exactly where a project is working — in a well-explored series, where every remaining question is a cliff. That is why models plateau: the easy structure–activity relationship is learned early, and what is left is the part that violates the assumption the model is built on.

What follows for you

Expect similarity-based methods to be good at triage over diverse libraries and poor at fine discrimination within a series — which is unfortunate, because the second is what medicinal chemists want most. Say so up front rather than discovering it in a review. Volume 15 carries the fuller treatment of cliffs as a field insight.

Choosing

Lesson 13

Which representation, when

SituationUseWhy
Predicting a physical property (solubility, LogP, melting point)Descriptors, or descriptors + fingerprintsThese properties are genuinely driven by bulk properties like size and polarity, which descriptors capture directly and interpretably.
Predicting binding to a specific proteinFingerprintsBinding depends on specific substructures in specific arrangements. Descriptors are too coarse.
Similarity search / virtual screeningMorgan fingerprintsPurpose-built for it, and fast in bulk.
Clustering or diversity selectionMorgan fingerprintsSame reason — plus Butina clustering expects them.
You need to explain the model to a chemistDescriptors, or MACCS"Too greasy" is a sentence. "Bit 1337 is high" is not.
You have > 50,000 data pointsLearned representations (Volume 10)Graph networks start to beat fixed fingerprints only with substantial data.
You have < 1,000 data pointsFingerprints + random forestThis is most real projects, and it is very hard to beat.
The default that wins more often than it should

Morgan radius 2, 2048 bits, plus about ten physicochemical descriptors, into a random forest. Build that first, always. It takes twenty minutes, it is hard to beat on typical dataset sizes, and it is the number every fancier approach has to justify itself against.
