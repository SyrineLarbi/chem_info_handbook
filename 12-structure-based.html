Part I

The target

Lesson 01

What a protein is, for our purposes

You do not need biochemistry to do this work, but you do need a working picture. Here is the minimum that makes everything else make sense.

  THE FOUR LEVELS

    PRIMARY      the sequence of amino acids. A string, like SMILES.
                 ...ALA-GLY-SER-LEU-TRP-ASP...

    SECONDARY    local shapes the chain folds into:
                 alpha helices (coils) and beta sheets (flat strands)

    TERTIARY     the whole chain folded into one 3D shape.
                 ⭐ THIS IS THE ONE THAT MATTERS TO US.

    QUATERNARY   several folded chains assembled together


  WHAT WE ACTUALLY CARE ABOUT: THE BINDING SITE

    Somewhere on that folded shape is a POCKET — a dent lined
    with amino acid side chains. A drug is a molecule shaped to
    sit in that dent and stay there.

         protein surface
        ~~~~~~~~~\        /~~~~~~~~~~
                  \      /
                   \    /     <- the pocket
                    \__/
                    [LIG]     <- your molecule, sitting in it

  ⭐ THE WHOLE OF STRUCTURE-BASED DESIGN IS ONE QUESTION:
     does this molecule fit that dent, and does it stay?
The twenty side chains, grouped the useful way

You do not need to memorise all twenty amino acids, but you do need the grouping, because it tells you what kind of molecule a pocket wants. Greasy (Leu, Ile, Val, Phe, Trp, Met) — a pocket lined with these wants a greasy part of your molecule. Polar (Ser, Thr, Asn, Gln, Tyr) — these make hydrogen bonds. Charged (Asp, Glu negative; Lys, Arg positive) — these make salt bridges, and they are the strongest single interactions you get. Special (Gly, tiny and flexible; Pro, rigid and kinks the chain; Cys, forms bridges; His, which can be charged or not depending on pH — and that ambiguity causes more docking errors than any other residue).

Lesson 02

Where structures come from, and how much to trust them

SourceWhat it isHow much to trust it
X-ray crystallographyThe classic experimental method. The PDB holds roughly 220,000 structures.Check the resolution. Below 2.0 Å is good; 2.0–2.5 Å is usable; above 3.0 Å, side-chain positions are guesses and you should not dock into it without care.
Cryo-EMIncreasingly common, especially for large complexes and membrane proteins.Resolution varies widely across one structure. Check the local resolution at your binding site, not the headline number.
NMRSolution structures, usually delivered as an ensemble of models.Useful for flexibility information. Pick a model deliberately, or use the ensemble.
AlphaFoldPredicted. AlphaFold2 solved single-chain prediction; AlphaFold3 extended it to complexes.Depends entirely on pLDDT — see below. Transformative for targets with no structure, dangerous when used carelessly.
Worked example · deciding whether to dock into an AlphaFold model 
pLDDT is a per-residue confidence score, 0-100, stored in the
B-factor column of the PDB file.

    > 90     very high — backbone AND side chains reliable
    70 - 90  confident — backbone good, side chains less so
    50 - 70  low — treat with caution
    < 50     very low — usually a disordered region, not a shape

THE CHECK THAT MATTERS

    Do NOT look at the average pLDDT for the whole protein.
    A protein can average 88 and still have a binding site at 55.

    1. list the residues lining your binding site
    2. read pLDDT for THOSE residues only
    3. if the binding-site mean is < 70, do not dock into it

⭐ AND THE LIMITATION NO pLDDT SCORE TELLS YOU

   AlphaFold predicts the APO structure — the protein WITHOUT a
   ligand. Many binding sites change shape when something binds
   (induced fit). A perfect 95-pLDDT apo structure can still have
   a pocket in the wrong conformation for your molecule.

   So: an experimental holo structure (protein WITH a ligand
   bound) beats a perfect prediction, every time.
Lesson 03

Protein preparation — the step everyone skips

A PDB file is not ready to use. Downloading it and docking straight into it is the most common beginner error in this entire volume, and it silently produces plausible, wrong answers.

  WHAT IS WRONG WITH A RAW PDB FILE

  1. NO HYDROGENS
       X-ray crystallography does not see hydrogen atoms. They
       are simply absent. Hydrogen bonds are THE key interaction,
       and you cannot detect one without hydrogens.
       -> you must add them, computationally

  2. PROTONATION STATES UNDECIDED
       Is that histidine positively charged or neutral? Which of
       its two nitrogens carries the hydrogen? Is that aspartate
       ionised?
       -> at pH 7.4 the answer is usually predictable, but
          HISTIDINE IS GENUINELY AMBIGUOUS and must be checked
          BY HAND for binding-site residues

  3. MISSING RESIDUES
       Flexible loops often do not appear in the density and are
       simply missing from the file. If one borders your pocket,
       your pocket has a hole in it that does not exist in reality.

  4. WATERS, IONS, BUFFER JUNK
       All mixed together. Some waters are structurally important;
       most are not. Glycerol and sulfate from the crystallisation
       buffer are not part of the protein.

  5. THE ASYMMETRIC UNIT IS NOT THE BIOLOGICAL MOLECULE
       ⚠ The file may contain a crystallographic repeating unit,
       not the real assembly. A "pocket" formed between two
       symmetry-related copies DOES NOT EXIST IN SOLUTION.
       -> always use the BIOLOGICAL ASSEMBLY file
The crystal-packing trap, concretely

A pocket-detection tool reports a beautiful, deep, druggable cavity. You design molecules for it for three months. It turns out the cavity is the gap between two protein copies packed against each other in the crystal — an artefact of how the protein was frozen, not a feature of the protein. The check is one click: download the biological assembly rather than the asymmetric unit.

Part II

Docking

Lesson 04

What docking actually does

Docking is two things bolted together, and almost every misunderstanding comes from confusing them.

  DOCKING = SEARCH + SCORE

  1. THE SEARCH (posing)
       Try to place the ligand in the pocket in many positions,
       orientations and internal shapes. Millions of candidate
       poses, explored by a search algorithm.

       Question answered: HOW might it sit?

  2. THE SCORE
       For each pose, compute a number meant to approximate
       binding strength, in kcal/mol. More negative = better.

       Question answered: HOW WELL does it sit?

  OUTPUT: a ranked list of poses with scores.

  ⭐ THE TWO ARE INDEPENDENTLY FALLIBLE. The search can miss the
     right pose entirely. Or it can find the right pose and the
     score can rank it fourth. When people say "docking failed",
     they usually have not worked out which half failed.
The search box — a practical detail that ruins results

You tell the docking program where to look with a box. Too small and the correct pose is literally outside the search space and can never be found; the program will confidently return the best of a bad set. Extend the box at least 5 Å beyond the binding site in every direction. And avoid "blind docking" across the whole protein when you can — it has far higher false-positive rates. Find candidate pockets first with a detection tool, then dock into each one separately.

Lesson 05

Scoring, and its real error

The number you must know

Docking scores carry a typical error of 2–3 kcal/mol. That is not a footnote — it determines everything you are allowed to claim.

What that error means in practice 
A score of -10.5 kcal/mol, with ±2-3 kcal/mol error,
is consistent with a real affinity anywhere from
about 1 nM to about 100 uM.

That is a range of FIVE ORDERS OF MAGNITUDE —
the difference between a drug and a non-binder.
What juniors say

"This compound scores −10.5, so it is a low-nanomolar binder." Or worse: comparing two analogues that differ by 0.4 kcal/mol and drawing an SAR conclusion. Medicinal-chemistry SAR differences are typically 0.5–1 kcal/mol, well inside the noise. You are reading the error bar.

What docking is legitimately for

Enrichment — concentrating actives near the top of a big list so you test 2,000 compounds instead of 500,000 — and binding-mode hypotheses, which are qualitative and testable. Not affinity prediction. Not SAR.

Lesson 06

Validating before you trust

Before a docking setup is used to find new actives, it must be shown to find known ones. This is retrospective validation, it takes an afternoon, and skipping it is how three-month screens end up meaningless.

Worked example · a retrospective validation 
SETUP
  50 known actives for this target (from ChEMBL)
+ 2,500 decoys — molecules with SIMILAR PHYSICAL PROPERTIES
  but presumed inactive (DUD-E builds these, or use property-
  matched randoms)
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  ⭐ WHY PROPERTY-MATCHED: if your decoys are all tiny and your
     actives are all large, you will "enrich" perfectly by
     measuring molecular weight. The test must be hard.

RUN the whole pipeline. Then:

  ROC-AUC         0.5 = random, 1.0 = perfect
                  > 0.7 is usable for screening
                  ⚠ but AUC weights the whole list equally, and
                    you only ever look at the top

  EF at 1%        enrichment factor in the top 1%
                  "how many times more actives than chance"
                  EF = 10 means 10x better than random
                  ⭐ THIS IS THE ONE THAT MATTERS, because it
                     describes the only part of the list you
                     will actually buy and test

IF EF@1% IS NEAR 1
    your setup cannot find actives it has already been given.
    It will not find new ones. Fix the setup — protonation,
    box size, waters, protein conformation — or use a
    ligand-based method instead (Volume 09).
Lesson 07

Reading a pose

The number that calibrates your confidence

The top-scoring pose is the experimentally correct binding mode roughly 50–70% of the time. So treating the top pose as "the answer" is a coin flip dressed as a result. Cluster the poses, look at several, and judge them chemically.

  HOW TO LOOK AT A POSE — the chemist's eye, in five checks

  1. DO THE HYDROGEN BONDS POINT THE RIGHT WAY?
       A donor must point AT an acceptor. Two acceptors facing
       each other is not an interaction, it is a clash — and
       scoring functions miss this more often than you expect.

  2. IS ANYTHING BURIED THAT SHOULD NOT BE?
       A charged group buried in a greasy pocket with nothing to
       pair with costs a large amount of energy. If the pose puts
       a carboxylate in an all-leucine pocket, the pose is wrong.

  3. IS THE LIGAND IN A SENSIBLE INTERNAL SHAPE?
       Docking programs can return strained conformations —
       eclipsed bonds, distorted rings. Compare the pose's
       internal energy against a relaxed conformer.

  4. DOES IT REPRODUCE THE KNOWN BINDING MODE?
       If a crystal structure of a related ligand exists, does
       your pose put the shared substructure in the same place?
       This is the strongest single check available.

  5. DOES IT AGREE WITH THE SAR?
       If chemistry says that adding a methyl here kills activity,
       does the pose explain why? A pose that contradicts known
       SAR is more likely wrong than the SAR is.
Part III

The interactions

Try it · A colleague reports "compound X docks at −11.2 kcal/mol, compound Y at −10.4, so X is the better binder — let's make X." What do you say?
Answer

That 0.8 kcal/mol difference is a third of the scoring function's own error. There is no information in it.

THE ARITHMETIC
   docking score error   ~2-3 kcal/mol
   the difference quoted  0.8 kcal/mol

   The gap between the two compounds is well inside the noise.
   Ranking them is reading the error bar.

AND THE SECOND PROBLEM
   -11.2 is being treated as an affinity. With +/- 2-3
   kcal/mol it is consistent with anything from roughly 1 nM
   to 100 uM — five orders of magnitude.

What to say, without being obstructive: "both are in the top tier, and docking cannot separate them. Let's make both if we can, or pick on something docking is actually good at — do the poses make chemical sense, does either explain the known SAR, and which is easier to synthesise?"

And the constructive close: if the project genuinely needs these two ranked, that is the argument for FEP (Lesson 14) — which is what it exists for, on a validated binding mode.

Lesson 08

The forces that hold a drug in place

Hydrogen bond
The most important directional interaction. Donor–acceptor distance 2.5–3.5 Å (optimal ~2.8), D–H⋯A angle above 120° (optimal 160–180°). Worth 1–5 kcal/mol each. Because it is directional, it provides selectivity, not just affinity.
Hydrophobic contact
Greasy surfaces packing against greasy surfaces. Individually weak and non-directional, but there are many of them and together they usually supply most of the binding energy.
Salt bridge
A charged group against an oppositely charged one — a ligand amine against an aspartate, say. The strongest single interaction available, and the reason ligand protonation state matters so much.
π-stacking
Aromatic rings stacked face-to-face or edge-to-face. Common with Phe, Tyr, Trp and His.
Halogen bond
A chlorine, bromine or iodine acting as an electron acceptor. Easily missed because it is counter-intuitive, and it is a real medicinal-chemistry tool.
Why ligand protonation is not a detail

A basic amine is protonated and positively charged at pH 7.4. Protonated, it forms a salt bridge with a nearby aspartate — a powerful interaction. Neutral, it forms a weak hydrogen bond at best. Docking everything in its neutral form, which is the default in many pipelines, therefore throws away the strongest interaction in the site for a large fraction of drug-like molecules. Generate states at pH 7.4 with a tool built for it.

Lesson 09

Interaction fingerprints — turning a pose into features

This is where structure-based work connects back to the machine learning of Volumes 08–10. A pose is a 3D object; a model wants a vector. An interaction fingerprint is the bridge.

Worked example · what an interaction fingerprint encodes 
A normal fingerprint (Volume 05) describes THE MOLECULE.
An interaction fingerprint describes THE COMPLEX —
what the molecule DOES against this specific protein.

For each binding-site residue, record which interaction types
are present:

                    HBd  HBa  Hydroph  PiStack  Ionic  Halogen
  ASP189             0    1      0        0       1      0
  SER190             1    1      0        0       0      0
  VAL213             0    0      1        0       0      0
  TRP215             0    0      1        1       0      0
  GLY216             1    1      0        0       0      0
  ...

  -> flatten to a bit vector: 010010 110000 001000 001100 ...

WHAT THIS IS GOOD FOR

  · comparing binding modes: two ligands with near-identical
    interaction fingerprints bind the same way, even if their
    2D structures look nothing alike
  · clustering docking poses by MECHANISM rather than geometry
  · as ML features, when you have a structure and want the model
    to see the interaction pattern rather than just the ligand

TOOLS: ProLIF (python, works on trajectories too), PLIP (quick,
       web or command line)

⭐ THE CAVEAT: an interaction fingerprint of a DOCKED pose
   inherits every error in that pose. Garbage pose, garbage
   fingerprint — and it will look just as tidy.
Lesson 10

Water

Beginners delete all the water molecules before docking because they look like clutter. Sometimes that is right. Sometimes it destroys the result.

What waters are doing

Some water molecules sit in the pocket permanently and mediate binding — the ligand hydrogen-bonds to the water, and the water hydrogen-bonds to the protein. Delete it and you have opened an artificial hole that your ligand will be docked into.

How to tell which are which

Conserved waters appear in the same place across multiple independent crystal structures of the same protein. That is the test. A water present in one structure at 2.8 Å resolution is probably noise; a water present in nine structures is part of the site.

What to do

Identify conserved waters, keep them, and — when it matters — dock both with and without them and compare. Being able to say "I ran it both ways and the binding mode was unchanged" is a much stronger position than having quietly picked one.

The physics underneath

Lesson 11

Conformers — the quiet dependency of everything 3D

A molecule is not one shape. Rotatable bonds let it fold into many, and almost every 3D method — docking, shape similarity, pharmacophore search, 3D descriptors — silently depends on whether you generated the right ones.

Worked example · how many conformers is enough 
THE SCALE OF THE PROBLEM

  Roughly 3 energy minima per rotatable bond, so a molecule with
  N rotatable bonds has on the order of 3^N conformers.

      N = 3   ->      27
      N = 5   ->     243
      N = 8   ->   6,561      <- a typical drug
      N = 12  -> 531,441      <- a floppy molecule

  You cannot enumerate them all, and you do not need to: most
  are high-energy shapes the molecule never adopts.

THE STANDARD RECIPE (RDKit)

  1. EMBED with ETKDG
       "Experimental Torsion Distance Geometry with basic
        Knowledge" — it biases the geometry using torsion angle
        preferences measured from crystal structures, which is
        why it beats plain distance geometry.

  2. OPTIMISE with a force field (MMFF94)
  3. DISCARD anything more than ~10 kcal/mol above the minimum
  4. DEDUPLICATE by RMSD (~0.5 A threshold)
  5. RANK by energy

HOW MANY TO ASK FOR
      rigid molecules        ~50
      typical drugs          200
      flexible molecules     200-500

⭐ THE FAILURE MODE THAT IS INVISIBLE
   If the bioactive conformation — the shape the molecule
   actually adopts when bound — is not in your generated set,
   docking CANNOT find the right pose. It will still return a
   confident score for the best wrong shape. Nothing in the
   output tells you this happened.

   And note: the bioactive conformer is often NOT the lowest
   energy one. Binding pays for some internal strain.
Lesson 12

Force fields and quantum mechanics

Force field (molecular mechanics)Quantum mechanics
The modelAtoms are balls, bonds are springs. Energy from a parameterised formula — bond stretching, angle bending, torsions, van der Waals, electrostatics.Solve an approximation to the electronic structure. Electrons are modelled explicitly.
SpeedMilliseconds. Millions of molecules is routine.Seconds to hours per molecule, depending on method and size.
Can doGeometry optimisation, conformers, MD, docking scoring.Reaction energies, bond breaking, accurate charges, spectra, excited states.
Cannot doAnything involving bonds breaking or forming. No electrons, no reactions. And it is only as good as its parameters — unusual chemistry is often unparameterised.Scale. And it will happily give you a beautifully accurate answer for a gas-phase molecule that has nothing to do with the protein.
The practical middle: xTB

Between the two sits semi-empirical quantum chemistry, of which xTB is the current workhorse. It is orders of magnitude faster than proper QM and much more transferable than a force field, which makes it the sensible default for things like conformer refinement, reasonable partial charges and tautomer ranking on thousands of molecules. When someone asks for "QM accuracy" on a screening library, xTB is usually the honest answer.

Lesson 13

Molecular dynamics, in one lesson

  WHAT MD DOES

    Docking gives you ONE FROZEN PICTURE.
    Reality is a MOVIE: protein and ligand both wobbling,
    water moving, side chains rotating, loops breathing.

    MD computes forces on every atom, moves them a tiny step
    (1-2 femtoseconds), and repeats — millions of times.

    1 femtosecond step x 100,000,000 steps = 100 nanoseconds
    ...which is still a very short time in biology.

  WHAT IT IS GENUINELY GOOD FOR

    · is the docked pose STABLE? run 100 ns and see whether the
      ligand stays put or drifts out. A pose that leaves the
      pocket in 20 ns was a bad pose.
    · which interactions PERSIST? an H-bond present in 85% of
      frames is real; one present in 4% was a lucky snapshot.
    · where are the conserved waters, dynamically?
    · does the pocket open, close, or change shape?

  WHAT IT IS NOT

    ⚠ NOT an affinity calculation. Watching a ligand stay bound
      does not tell you how strongly. That is FEP's job.

  THE COST
    Setup is fiddly (force field for the ligand, solvation, ions,
    equilibration) and a single 100 ns run is hours to days on a
    GPU. This is a tens-of-molecules technique, not thousands.
Lesson 14

FEP — the gold standard, and when it is worth it

DockingFEP
SpeedSeconds per moleculeHours to days per molecule
Ranking accuracyPoor — R² around 0.1–0.3Good — R² around 0.5–0.7
ScaleScreening millionsOptimising tens
NeedsProtein plus a binding siteProtein plus a validated binding mode
OutputRanked list and posesRelative ΔG between similar ligands
CostFree (AutoDock Vina)Commercial (FEP+) or complex to run (OpenFE)
Worked example · the trick that makes FEP possible 
THE HARD QUESTION
    "What is the absolute binding free energy of ligand A?"
    -> extremely difficult, and rarely answered well.

THE TRACTABLE QUESTION
    "Is ligand B better than ligand A, and by how much?"
    -> this is what FEP answers.

THE TRICK: ALCHEMY

    Rather than pulling A out of the protein (hard), slowly
    MORPH A into B while it stays bound — turning atoms on and
    off gradually in a simulation. Do the same morph in plain
    water. Then:

      ddG = dG(complex, A->B) - dG(solvent, A->B)

    The morph is physically impossible, which does not matter:
    free energy is a state function, so only the endpoints count.

⭐ WHEN IT IS WORTH IT
   Lead optimisation, on a series where you already have a
   confirmed binding mode and are choosing which 20 analogues to
   synthesise. Getting the ranking right saves months of
   chemistry, which easily pays for a week of compute.

⭐ WHEN IT IS NOT
   Early screening. Different chemical series (FEP compares
   similar molecules; morphing A into something structurally
   unrelated does not converge). And never on an unvalidated
   binding mode — FEP applied to the wrong pose gives you a
   precise, well-converged, entirely wrong number.
Part V

Designing with structure

Lesson 15

Pharmacophores and fragments

Pharmacophore — the abstraction

Strip away the chemistry and keep only the pattern of features in space: a hydrogen-bond donor here, an aromatic ring 5.2 Å away, a positive charge 7.8 Å from that. Any molecule that can place those features at those distances is a candidate — however different it looks.

Why that is powerful

It is the one method that reliably finds scaffold hops — a molecule with a completely different core that binds the same way. Fingerprint similarity (Volume 05) cannot do this by construction, because it compares substructures and there are none in common.

Fragment-based design — the other direction

Start with very small molecules (under ~250 Da). They bind weakly — micromolar to millimolar, which needs sensitive biophysical methods to detect at all — but they bind efficiently, making good contacts per atom. Then grow, merge or link them into a real lead. It explores chemical space far more efficiently than screening whole drug-sized molecules, because small fragments cover far more of it.

The metric to know

Ligand efficiency — binding energy divided by heavy-atom count. It is how fragment work is judged, and it exists because raw potency rewards adding mass, which is exactly the habit that produces bloated, undruggable leads.

Lesson 16

Finding the pocket in the first place

Everything above assumed you know where the binding site is. Sometimes you do — a ligand is bound in the structure. When you do not, pocket detection comes first, and it is the step that decides whether the rest is meaningful.

ToolMethodNotes
fpocketAlpha spheres — packs spheres into surface concavities and clusters themOpen source, fast, scriptable. The sensible default, and easy to run across many structures.
DoGSiteScorerGrid-based, using a difference-of-Gaussians filterAdds a druggability score and subpocket segmentation — the latter genuinely useful for fragment work. Web service at proteins.plus; results can be downloaded and parsed.
SiteMapCommercial, produces site mapsIts lasting contribution is the concept: coloured surfaces showing where a hydrophobic group, a donor or an acceptor would each be favourable — a pharmacophore-like read of an empty pocket.
What "druggable" means here

Not "a drug exists for it" but a geometry claim: buried enough, hydrophobic enough, and the right size — roughly 300–1,000 Å³ — to hold a small molecule tightly. A shallow polar groove may be a real functional site and still be undruggable by a small molecule.

The two traps

The top-ranked pocket is frequently the active site of some other function, or a crystal-packing artefact (Lesson 03). Rank several, and check each against what is known about the protein's biology before designing anything for it.

The habit

If a ligand-bound structure exists anywhere in the PDB for this protein or a close homologue, use its ligand position rather than a prediction. Detection is for when you have no such structure, not a substitute for looking.

Reference

Rapid drill

One-sentence answers to have ready
What does docking compute?
  Two things: a search for plausible poses, and a score for each.
  They fail independently.

What is a docking score's error?
  2-3 kcal/mol — which spans about five orders of magnitude in
  affinity. So: enrichment and binding modes, never affinity.

Why can't I use docking scores for SAR?
  SAR differences are 0.5-1 kcal/mol, smaller than the error.
  You would be reading noise.

How do I know my docking setup works?
  Retrospective validation: known actives plus property-matched
  decoys, and report enrichment at 1%, not just ROC-AUC.

What is missing from a raw PDB file?
  Hydrogens, protonation states, sometimes whole loops — and it
  may be the asymmetric unit rather than the biological assembly.

When can I dock into an AlphaFold structure?
  When pLDDT in the BINDING SITE residues is above ~70 — not the
  whole-protein average. And remember it predicts the apo form.

Should I delete the waters?
  Not the conserved ones. Conserved means present across multiple
  independent structures. Dock both ways and compare.

How many conformers?
  ~50 rigid, 200 typical, 200-500 flexible. If the bioactive
  conformer is not in the set, docking cannot succeed — and
  nothing in the output will tell you.

Force field or QM?
  Force field for anything at scale; QM when bonds break or you
  need real electronic structure. xTB is the practical middle.

Docking or FEP?
  Docking to screen millions (R2 0.1-0.3). FEP to rank tens
  (R2 0.5-0.7), and only on a validated binding mode.

What is ligand efficiency for?
  Binding energy per heavy atom — it stops you rewarding
  molecules for simply getting bigger.
