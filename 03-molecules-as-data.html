AI cheminformatics · Volume 03 of 17 · phase 1

How a molecule becomes data. The notation everything else is built on.

Before you can compute anything about a molecule you have to write it down in a way a computer can read without ambiguity. That turns out to be the deepest problem in the field — because the same substance can be written many ways, and two of your rows being "the same molecule" is a question with a surprisingly slippery answer.

Part I

SMILES

Five lessons. By the end you will read them fluently enough to debug data, which is the actual skill being taught.

Lesson 01

The five rules that cover 90% of SMILES

SMILES — Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System — writes a molecular graph as a line of text by walking the atoms and recording what it passes. Five rules cover nearly everything:

The five rules
1. ATOMS ARE LETTERS
   C N O S P F Cl Br I      written bare — the "organic subset"
   anything else            in square brackets: [Na+] [Fe] [Si]
   HYDROGENS ARE INVISIBLE  — assumed to fill each atom's remaining valence

2. BONDS ARE SYMBOLS BETWEEN THEM
   (nothing)  single        CC        ethane
   =          double        C=C       ethene
   #          triple        C#C       ethyne
   :          aromatic      rarely written by hand — implied by lowercase

3. BRANCHES GO IN ROUND BRACKETS
   CC(C)C          isobutane — a chain of C, with a C hanging off the second
   CC(=O)O         acetic acid — the (=O) branches off, then the walk continues to O

4. RINGS ARE MATCHED DIGITS
   C1CCCCC1        cyclohexane — the atom labelled 1 bonds to the atom that closes 1
   the digit is a LABEL, not a count, and is reused freely once closed

5. DOTS SEPARATE DISCONNECTED PIECES
   [Na+].[Cl-]     sodium chloride — two species, one string
   CC(=O)O.O       acetic acid with a water of crystallisation

⭐ EVERYTHING ELSE — aromaticity, charge, stereochemistry — IS AN
   ELABORATION OF THESE FIVE.
Worked example · building caffeine up from nothing

Caffeine is Cn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C — the point where beginners give up. Walk it slowly and it is just the five rules.

Cn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C

C           a methyl carbon, hanging off what comes next
n1          an aromatic NITROGEN, opening ring 1
c n c2      aromatic carbon, nitrogen, then a carbon that ALSO opens ring 2
                -> this atom is in two rings at once: the rings are FUSED
c1          closes ring 1 back to the n1 atom
c(=O)       aromatic carbon carrying a double-bonded oxygen  (a C=O)
n(C)        aromatic nitrogen with a methyl branch
c(=O)       another C=O
n2          closes ring 2
C           a final methyl on that nitrogen

RESULT: two fused rings, four nitrogens, three methyls, two C=O groups.
        Which is caffeine.

The one genuinely new idea is the fused ring. An atom that opens two ring numbers belongs to two rings — that is how SMILES expresses a fused system without any special syntax. Once you see c2...c1...n2 as "open 2, close 1, close 2" the string stops being intimidating.

Lesson 02

Aromaticity — why some letters are lowercase

You will see benzene written two ways, and both are valid:

WrittenCalledWhat it says
c1ccccc1Aromatic form"These six atoms are aromatic." The bonds are neither single nor double — the electrons are delocalised around the ring.
C1=CC=CC=C1Kekulé form"Alternating single and double bonds." A specific, and slightly fictional, picture of the same ring.

Both parse to the same molecule in RDKit, because on reading it runs a step called sanitisation that includes perceiving aromaticity — it works out which rings are aromatic and marks them, regardless of how you wrote it. This is why your typed SMILES often comes back looking different.

Where this becomes a real problem

Different toolkits perceive aromaticity differently. RDKit, Open Babel and commercial toolkits use different models, and they disagree on edge cases — certain fused rings, some charged heterocycles. A molecule round-tripped through two toolkits can come back as a different string, and any comparison you do by string then fails.

The defence: pick one toolkit for canonicalisation, do it once at ingestion, and store the result. Never compare SMILES produced by different tools. Lesson 05 is the mechanism.

Lesson 03

Charges, brackets and explicit hydrogens

Square brackets mean "I am specifying this atom exactly — do not infer." Inside them, everything becomes explicit:

Reading a bracket atom
[C@@H](N)(C)C=O          a bracket atom carries, in order:

  isotope   element   chirality   H count   charge
    13         C         @@         H1        +
   [13C@@H+]   — all optional, all in that order

EXAMPLES YOU WILL ACTUALLY MEET:
  [NH4+]      ammonium — nitrogen, 4 hydrogens, +1 charge
  [O-]        an oxygen carrying a negative charge
  [nH]        an aromatic nitrogen with an explicit hydrogen on it
                 ** this one matters enormously — see below **
  [Na+].[Cl-] a salt, as two disconnected charged pieces
  [13C]       carbon-13, an isotope label
The [nH] trap

In an aromatic ring, a nitrogen may or may not carry a hydrogen, and the two are different molecules. Pyrrole is c1cc[nH]c1; pyridine is c1ccncc1. Write n where you meant [nH] and the valence does not work out — RDKit returns None, and the error message does not say "you forgot a hydrogen".

This is the most common reason a hand-typed heterocycle fails to parse. If a five-membered aromatic ring with a nitrogen will not parse, try [nH] first.

Lesson 04

Stereochemistry — the same graph, different molecules

Two molecules can have identical atoms and identical bonds and still be different substances, because the atoms are arranged differently in space. This is not a technicality: it is the difference between a drug and a poison, and getting it wrong in a dataset is a real error.

Tetrahedral chirality — @ and @@

A carbon with four different groups can be arranged two ways that are mirror images and not superimposable — like your left and right hands. SMILES writes these as @ and @@.

N[C@@H](C)C(=O)O and N[C@H](C)C(=O)O are the two forms of alanine. Same atoms, same bonds, mirror images.

Double bond geometry — / and \

Groups on a double bond cannot rotate past each other, so they are fixed on the same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans).

F/C=C/F is trans; F/C=C\F is cis. The slashes describe the bonds around the double bond, not the double bond itself — which is why they look confusing, and why they come in pairs.

Why this hurts in practice

Public datasets are inconsistent about stereochemistry. The same compound appears with full stereo in one source, flat in another. If you deduplicate by SMILES, the flat and stereo forms count as different compounds. If you strip stereo to make them match, you merge two genuinely different substances — with different activities — into one row.

There is no universally right answer. There is only a decision, recorded: either keep stereo and accept duplicates, or strip it consistently across the whole dataset and document that you did.

The one to remember

Thalidomide. One mirror image treated morning sickness; the other caused severe birth defects. It is the field's standing reminder that @ versus @@ is not a formatting detail.

Lesson 05

Canonical SMILES — the one that makes comparison possible

Here is the problem in one line. All of these are ethanol:

Worked example · one molecule, many strings 
CCO        OCC        C(C)O       [CH3][CH2][OH]

Four valid SMILES. One substance. If you dedupe your dataset by string
comparison, you keep all four and your "3,000 unique compounds" is a lie.

THE FIX — canonicalisation:

  from rdkit import Chem

  for s in ["CCO", "OCC", "C(C)O", "[CH3][CH2][OH]"]:
      print(s, "->", Chem.MolToSmiles(Chem.MolFromSmiles(s)))

  CCO              -> CCO
  OCC              -> CCO
  C(C)O            -> CCO
  [CH3][CH2][OH]   -> CCO        <-- all four now identical

What happened: the string was parsed into a graph, and the graph was written back out using a deterministic algorithm that always starts in the same place and walks in the same order. The output is the canonical SMILES. Two molecules are the same if and only if their canonical SMILES match.

The standing rule from here on: canonicalise at ingestion, store the canonical form, and compare only canonical forms. Never compare raw input strings.

The caveat that matters

Canonical SMILES is canonical within one toolkit and one version. RDKit's canonical form is not Open Babel's, and RDKit has changed its algorithm between major versions. So canonical SMILES is an excellent working key inside one project and a poor durable key across projects and years. For that you want the InChIKey — Lesson 07.

Try it · You inherit a dataset of 5,000 rows claiming 5,000 unique compounds. What three checks tell you the real number, in order of how much they will find?
Answer
  1. Canonicalise and count uniques. Parse every SMILES, write it back with MolToSmiles, count distinct. This alone often removes 2–10% on data assembled from several sources.
  2. Strip salts and count again. CC(=O)O.[Na+] and CC(=O)O are the sodium salt and the free acid — different strings, and for most modelling purposes the same compound. Volume 06 covers the standardisation that handles this.
  3. Canonicalise tautomers and count again. The subtlest and last, because it is the one with genuine judgement in it — see Lesson 10.

Report all four numbers: raw rows, unique canonical, unique after desalting, unique after tautomer canonicalisation. The gap between the first and the last is a fact about your dataset that anyone using the model needs to know, and quoting only the raw count is how people accidentally overstate the size of their training data.

Lesson 06

SMARTS — the pattern language

SMILES describes a molecule. SMARTS describes a class of molecules. The relationship is exactly that of a string to a regular expression, and the analogy holds all the way down.

SMARTSMatchesUsed for
[OH]Any oxygen with a hydrogen on itFinding alcohols and acids
c1ccccc1Any benzene ring, anywhere in a moleculeSubstructure filtering
[C,N]Carbon or nitrogen — comma is ORLoosening a pattern
[!C]Anything that is not carbonFinding heteroatoms
[#6]Any carbon, aromatic or not, by atomic numberAvoiding the aromatic/aliphatic trap
[CX4]A carbon with exactly four connectionsSpecifying saturation
[R] / [!R]An atom in a ring / not in a ringDistinguishing chain from ring positions
C(=O)[OH]A carboxylic acid groupThe most-used pattern in the field
Worked example · finding every carboxylic acid 
from rdkit import Chem

pattern = Chem.MolFromSmarts("C(=O)[OH]")          # note: FromSmarts, not FromSmiles

for name, smi in [("aspirin",     "CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O"),
                  ("paracetamol", "CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1"),
                  ("caffeine",    "Cn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C")]:
    mol = Chem.MolFromSmiles(smi)
    print(name, mol.HasSubstructMatch(pattern))

  aspirin      True         <-- it has the acid
  paracetamol  False        <-- it has an OH, but on a ring, not an acid
  caffeine     False

# WHERE it matched, as atom indices:
mol = Chem.MolFromSmiles("CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O")
print(mol.GetSubstructMatches(pattern))     # ((8, 9, 10),)

Look at the paracetamol row. It contains an -OH, so a careless pattern of just [OH] would have matched it. The pattern C(=O)[OH] requires the OH to sit on a carbon that also carries a double-bonded oxygen — which is what makes it an acid rather than an alcohol. Precision in the pattern is the whole skill, and a too-loose SMARTS silently over-filters your library.

Where you will meet SMARTS in anger

Three places: PAINS filters (patterns for compounds that give false positives in assays — Volume 06), reaction encoding for retrosynthesis (Volume 11), and functional-group counting as model features. You will mostly use published SMARTS sets rather than writing your own, but you need to read them well enough to know what they exclude.

Part III

The other formats

Lesson 07

InChI and the InChIKey

InChI — International Chemical Identifier — was designed by IUPAC to solve the problem canonical SMILES only half solves: one substance, one string, produced by anyone, anywhere, forever. It is generated by a single reference implementation, so there is no toolkit-disagreement problem.

Worked example · aspirin's three identifiers 
SMILES (canonical, RDKit)
  CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O
  readable · toolkit-dependent · good working key

InChI
  InChI=1S/C9H8O4/c1-6(10)13-8-5-3-2-4-7(8)9(11)12/h2-5H,1H3,(H,11,12)
  unreadable · layered (formula / connections / hydrogens) · fully standard

InChIKey
  BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ-UHFFFAOYSA-N
  a fixed-length HASH of the InChI, 27 characters:

  BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ - UHFFFAOYSA - N
  |________|       |________|   |
  the skeleton     stereo and    protonation
  (connectivity)   other layers  flag

⭐ WHY THE SPLIT MATTERS: two molecules that differ ONLY in
   stereochemistry share the first 14 characters and differ after
   the hyphen. So you can search for "the same skeleton, any
   stereoisomer" with a prefix match.

What to use it for: the InChIKey is the right database key and the right thing to search a supplier catalogue or PubChem with. It is not human-readable and cannot be converted back into a structure, so you store it alongside the SMILES, never instead of it.

What InChI does not solve

InChI standardises the representation, not the chemistry. Two tautomers of one compound can still produce different InChIKeys, and drawing choices a chemist made still propagate. Standard InChI applies some tautomer normalisation, but it does not handle every case — so the tautomer problem in Lesson 10 survives InChI, and you still need the curation step.

Lesson 08

MOL, SDF and PDB — the file formats

FormatHoldsWhere it comes from
MOLOne molecule: an atom block with coordinates, a bond block, charges.The classic single-structure format. Has 2D or 3D coordinates.
SDFMany MOL blocks separated by $$$$, each with arbitrary named data fields.How real datasets arrive. Vendor catalogues, ChEMBL exports, screening results.
PDBProtein 3D structures — one line per atom, plus metadata.The Protein Data Bank. Volume 12 lives in this format.
PDBQTPDB plus partial charges and atom types.What AutoDock Vina eats. Volume 12.

What an SDF record actually looks like
  aspirin
       RDKit          2D
   13 13  0  0  0  0  0  0  0  0999 V2000     <-- 13 atoms, 13 bonds
      1.2990   -0.7500    0.0000 C   0  0      <-- x  y  z  element
      ... one line per atom ...
    1  2  1  0                                 <-- atom1 atom2 bondtype
      ... one line per bond ...
  M  END
  >  <compound_id>                             <-- DATA FIELDS start here
  CHEMBL25

  >  <IC50_nM>
  120

  >  <assay_id>
  CHEMBL829584

  $$$$                                         <-- end of record; next one follows
Why SDF matters more than it looks

The data fields are the reason SDF is still everywhere. A CSV of SMILES loses the 3D coordinates and the per-compound metadata; an SDF carries the structure, the conformation and any number of named properties in one file. When someone sends you "the screening results", it will be an SDF, and the fields will be inconsistently named. Read them with Chem.SDMolSupplier and inspect mol.GetPropsAsDict() on the first record before assuming anything.

Part IV

Where it gets hard

Lesson 10

The tautomer problem

What a tautomer is

Two structures that interconvert by moving a hydrogen and shifting a double bond, fast enough that in solution you have a mixture of both rather than two separable substances.

The classic case: a keto form CC(=O)CC(=O)C and its enol form CC(O)=CC(=O)C. Different graphs. Different SMILES. Different InChIKeys. Arguably the same compound sitting in a bottle.

Why it breaks things
  • Deduplication fails. The same compound from two sources, drawn as two tautomers, counts twice — with two possibly-different activity values.
  • Fingerprints differ. The bonds differ, so the fragments differ, so the model sees two unrelated molecules.
  • Substructure searches miss. A SMARTS written for the keto form does not match a record stored as the enol.
Why it cannot simply be solved

Because which tautomer dominates depends on conditions — solvent, pH, temperature — and on the protein it is binding to. There is no universally correct single answer, only a consistent convention.

What you actually do

Pick a canonical tautomer using a documented algorithm — RDKit's MolStandardize.rdMolStandardize.TautomerEnumerator has a Canonicalize method — apply it to every molecule at ingestion, and record that you did, with the RDKit version. The goal is not chemical truth. It is that identical compounds land on identical representations, consistently, across your whole dataset. Volume 06 puts this in the pipeline.

Lesson 11

2D, 3D and conformers

A SMILES has no coordinates at all — it is pure connectivity. Everything spatial has to be generated, and there are two very different levels of "spatial":

No coordinates

What a SMILES gives you. Enough for descriptors, fingerprints, substructure search and most machine learning. Most of this course lives here.

2D coordinates

A flat drawing, generated for display. AllChem.Compute2DCoords(mol). Aesthetic, not physical — never use them as features.

3D conformers

Real spatial positions, generated by embedding and then energy-minimising. Needed for docking and shape comparison. Slow, and not unique.

The conformer subtlety worth understanding early

A flexible molecule has no single 3D structure. A molecule with eight rotatable bonds has an enormous number of accessible shapes, and the one that binds to a protein is often not the lowest-energy one in isolation. So "generate the 3D structure" is not a well-posed request — you generate an ensemble of conformers and work with the ensemble.

This is why docking is hard (Volume 12), why 3D descriptors are used more cautiously than 2D ones, and why a surprising amount of successful cheminformatics ignores 3D entirely.
